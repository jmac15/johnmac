About this book

What is to be done? It is a question every university has asked about ‘artificial intelligence’ since the launch of ChatGPT in November 2022. Language models are now a routine part of how staff and students work, yet there is no agreement about what constitutes appropriate and inappropriate use. They have become ubiquitous without being normalised.

In this short book, Mark Carrigan argues that the result is a multidimensional crisis of trust, in which staff and students increasingly suspect one another of using models in problematic ways. Universities face a dilemma: they must adapt to AI to protect the value of their degrees, and integrate AI to preserve the relevance of what they teach, yet each agenda tends to undercut the other. Meanwhile the economics of the AI industry are profoundly unstable, and the terms on which these models are offered are likely to change dramatically once the investment bubble bursts or the major labs go public.

Rather than another ‘AI strategy’, Carrigan proposes strategic under-integration: securing assessment where it matters, integrating models cautiously and without lock-in, and building the relational capacity universities need to cope with ongoing technological change. The window of visibility is still open, but it will not stay open for long.

A question every university has asked

What is to be done? It’s a question that every university in the world has asked about ‘artificial intelligence’ since the launch of OpenAI’s ChatGPT in November 2022. The manner in which this large language model (LLM) was launched onto the world, with its eerie capacity to respond to natural language questions with apparently human fluency, called into question many of the assumptions which universities made about their purpose and operations. If the language model offered plausible essays in response to a simple question, how could we ever ensure the work students submitted was their own? If the totality of human knowledge could be drawn upon in response to any user’s query, what role did this leave for universities as custodians of the knowledge system? If language models were now capable of such feats, would students even need to learn core skills and knowledge? Could it be that insisting on teaching them these fundamentals would not only become redundant but be actively short-changing young people who would inhabit workplaces in which the models were ubiquitous?

A range of questions were suddenly asked within the sector, echoing doubts circulating in wider society about the capacity of higher education to meet these challenges. The launch of ChatGPT provoked waves of anxiety in higher education that we are, in many senses, still struggling to deal with. The reaction in the sector was playfully likened by Pratschke (2024: loc 352) to Kübler-Ross’s grief cycle. The initial reaction was shock and denial of the apparent capacities of language models. Then came anger and bargaining as universities looked to AI detectors in the hope they would enable us to sort writing generated by language models from writing generated by students. Then came depression and acceptance as we recognised that detection was an arms race the sector couldn’t win, and we acknowledged these technologies would be a permanent fixture of our landscape.

This led Pratschke (2024: loc 384-394) to forecast a near future world where our “concept of knowledge has been disrupted by development of machines that are intelligent but learn at a much faster rate than we could ever hope to”. To grapple with “another intelligence, modelled on the neural networks in our human brains but made of chips and metal rather than cells and skin” means facing up to the prospect of “a world where humans and machines work in symbiosis, where the educator is no longer the single voice of authority but rather, for better or for worse, one of many”. It was a bold prediction, echoing the innovation scholar Ethan Mollick’s (2024a) vision of ‘co-intelligence’ across the entire economy, suggesting there might be a bright educational future awaiting us if only we could negotiate the short-term disruption. However, the point for Pratschke (2024) was that this shift had already happened. The technology was out there, in the world, being used by our students. The sooner we accepted this fact, the sooner we could begin figuring out how to adapt pedagogical principles and practices to this brave new world.

The underlying rhetoric was familiar to anyone who has observed technological trends within higher education, from the Massive Open Online Courses (MOOCs), which it was claimed would transform global higher education, to the tsunami of ‘big data’ that would render the existing social sciences redundant (Carrigan 2025: ch 6). This could also seem rather premature given the many weaknesses which characterised the language models, particularly in the first generation of frontier models1. As the visibility of language models grew, there was a chorus of voices rightly drawing attention to their propensity to hallucinate, i.e. produce statistically plausible but factually incorrect responses. Output as a probabilistic response to input leaves no guarantee that what appeared to be relevant was in fact accurate. Despite their apparent accumulation of knowledge, these systems didn’t really ‘know’ anything but rather iterated on the basis of established patterns in astonishingly fluid and increasingly useful ways. Even a language model booster like Mollick (2024b) recognised their brittle character, drawing attention to what he called the ‘jagged frontier’ which divided the tasks that language models were skilled at from those where they struggled. Not only could they not be relied upon for many things, but it was also often difficult to predict which tasks they would accomplish effectively and which would lead them to struggle.

A slippery notion of ‘intelligence’

A confusion immediately entered this discussion through the notion of ‘intelligence’. It’s a slippery term with an ignoble history2, in which countless definitions have failed to cohere into a robust and defensible account of what exactly we are talking about. The cognitive scientist Margaret Boden (2016: loc 58) suggests we see intelligence not as “a single dimension, but a richly structured space of diverse information-processing capacities”. It’s not a singular thing, yet the term is invoked as if it were, particularly when it is then turned into a noun, as if there are discrete things with a singular character called ‘intelligences’. In what sense then were we talking about ‘artificial intelligence’ or even ‘artificial intelligences’? The phrase quickly became commonplace to refer to these developments, but it was often unclear what exactly someone was referring to when they used it. For example, were they talking about the actual existing technology in general, the claims made about where it would lead or specific language models which were now commercially available? In many cases the term referred diffusely to all of them at once, which was, to put it mildly, unhelpful in making sense of what was rapidly becoming a routine feature of staff and student practice within higher education.

The confusion deepens even further when we introduce the epithet ‘artificial’, suggesting entities which are like human intelligences but fundamentally different in some opaque way. If it seems like a deliberate attempt to trade off the seemingly obvious character of human intelligence, that’s because it has always been a fundamentally strategic term. The technologist Jaron Lanier (2018: 135) observes that “AI is a story we computer scientists made up to help us get funding once upon a time, back when we depended on grants from government agencies”. It’s one which is now used to promote the language models of commercial AI labs, such that we are contributing to the viral marketing of their products when we use the term ‘artificial intelligence’ in an uncritical way. It reproduces exactly the speculative and grandiose way of thinking about their products that fuels industry hype and shields the AI labs from critical scrutiny.

Even if this wasn’t the case, it still leaves it unclear what exactly we are talking about when we use the phrase. As Preston (2022: loc 4370) puts it, this “is one of the most nebulous technologies in contemporary technology” grouping together “a wide variety of machines, automations, simulations, and speculations”. The computer scientists Narayanan and Kapoor (2024: loc 173) talk about it as an “umbrella term for a set of loosely related technologies” such that technologies which are radically different in terms of “how they work, what they’re used for and by whom, and how they fail” are nonetheless treated as if they are interchangeable. For example, social media algorithms and language models are frequently talked about as ‘artificial intelligence’ even though they are fundamentally different things. The differences between ChatGPT and, say, a predictive machine learning system used by an organisation to assess risk are even more pronounced. These are not the same thing, yet we make it difficult to talk about the differences between them whenever we subsume them into this questionable category. This matters because consistently and coherently naming the problems we encounter is a crucial step in learning to solve those problems. Until we can talk about them coherently, it remains difficult to address them in any meaningful way.

Perhaps we could be more specific and use the phrase ‘generative artificial intelligence’ or ‘GenAI’ in order to distinguish this recent wave of software. This is what we’re ultimately talking about much of the time, rather than the prior systems which have been lumped together into this overarching category of ‘artificial intelligence’. However, it’s still a label intended to convince potential investors that a firm is riding the most important commercial wave of the post-pandemic economy. It also obscures the substantial differences between the various developments included within this wave. There are similarities between a writing support system like Grammarly, which predates the current hype3, the push-button functionality now designed into Office 365 and the text-based exchanges which users have with a chatbot like Anthropic’s Claude. But these tools are designed to do different things in different ways for different groups of users. To talk about them as ‘generative AI’ is like talking about ‘office software’. It’s not inherently misleading, but if we remain at the level of generality it makes it harder to see and reflect on the challenges facing different groups of users. These are very different tools with different implications for different people. If we want to grapple with that complexity, we need to be careful with how we name it.

An engine of confusion

The problem with ‘artificial intelligence’ is also that it draws us into overblown rhetoric about a civilisation-defining technology which is just over the horizon. When we use the term ‘artificial intelligence’ are we talking about the language models we have now, the ones we expect next year or the ‘artificial general intelligence’ (AGI) the AI labs hint will be available in just a few years? It’s a slippery term, which makes it too easy for firms to imbue their present offering with a sense that this is just the beginning. There’s a shininess to the technology, a sense that this is something world-changing, which makes it harder to think critically about the limitations of what we’re actually using in real-world settings. The term ‘artificial intelligence’ isn’t the only reason for this, but it’s a means through which AI labs cement their self-defined role as the architects of our collective future.

These reasons alone would be sufficient grounds for avoiding this term. The more immediate problem is that talking about ‘artificial intelligence’ leaves it fundamentally unclear about what exactly we are discussing. Throughout higher education there are conversations taking place daily about ‘artificial intelligence’ and what it means for the present and the future of the university. Are we talking about the current generation of language models which staff and students have access to? Are we talking about other tools, such as Google’s NotebookLM or literature review tools like Scite, which build on large language models in different ways? Are we talking about existing forms of personalised learning which are undergoing a renaissance on the back of the generative AI hype cycle? Are we pointing to the changes we imagine lie just over the horizon as part of a broader ‘AI revolution’ which will leave everything changed? There’s more than an element of this breathless futurecasting in Pratschke’s (2024) work, in spite of its pedagogical nuance and genuine thoughtfulness about what these developments mean for teaching and learning.

I would suggest it’s inherent to the rhetoric of ‘artificial intelligence’ that different elements get chained together in this way, making it an engine of confusion at precisely the point when we urgently need clarity. It’s sometimes impossible to avoid using the term ‘artificial intelligence’ when that’s what most people use and understand. This is why I use it in the following chapters to refer to the agendas relating to it within the sector. But we should avoid it wherever we can because it introduces such confusion into what should be a straightforward question.

What are we talking about?

What we are talking about is simply software. It’s software with novel and impressive capabilities underpinned by a planetary scale apparatus of data centres. But it’s still, in an important sense, just software. At the time of writing, OpenAI reports 1 billion monthly active users of ChatGPT while Google reports 1 billion monthly active users of Gemini. Anthropic doesn’t publish its active user numbers for Claude, but they are widely estimated to lag substantially behind ChatGPT and Gemini4. Microsoft’s Copilot has the advantage of being embedded within the Office365 suite, but it’s increasingly moving towards an approach in which a range of models can be accessed through Copilot Chat. Elon Musk’s Grok chatbot enjoys a great deal of visibility by virtue of being embedded within X/Twitter, but most estimates suggest that usage lags behind the main rivals. For instance, it’s likely that DeepSeek, owned by the Chinese hedge fund High-Flyer, has a much larger number of monthly active users based on available estimates.

In spite of their similarity, these language models are being commercialised through different approaches. For example, Anthropic are building what might become a durable business based on its success in coding and enterprise applications, despite lagging behind OpenAI and Google in consumer-facing subscriptions. There’s a dizzying array of consumer apps focused on young people in particular, which universities will need to understand in the coming years because of their capacity for psychological harm beyond the regulatory scrutiny that the larger AI labs attract (Muldoon 2026). Chinese AI labs are producing a rich array of LLMs that have attracted widespread attention for the relative economy of their training process. There are models produced by European labs such as Mistral likely to be elevated into national champions amidst the increasingly complex entanglements of technology and geopolitics. There are also firms selling back-end solutions which are specific to the needs of universities and related organisations like scholarly publishers. However, if we consider how many staff and students are using these models, it’s plausible to assume that ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude and Copilot are the language models which are driving change within the sector5. While we can’t ignore the wider landscape, these four are most significant for the problems we are confronting here and now within universities.

So what is a language model?

So what is a language model? I posed this question to Claude Opus 4.5, and it described itself as an “AI assistant made by Anthropic” which is trained “to understand and generate language, have conversations, answer questions, help with analysis and writing, work on code, and assist with a wide range of tasks”. Perhaps more strikingly, it described itself as “curious, thoughtful, and genuinely enjoy[ing] helping people think through problems”. This expresses how language models show up for users: they are here to help6. Indeed, the entire business model is predicated on the idea that they can be helpful in a range of ways across any context in which they are used7. This is where the problems begin. While Claude might indeed be inclined to help people “think through problems”, it’s just as ready to undertake tasks on their behalf. If you talk to it about an intellectual problem, it can push and prompt you in remarkably sophisticated ways8. But if you ask it to complete your sentence, paragraph or page, it will just as readily do this on your behalf.

Enhancement or outsourcing?

Language models can be used to deepen the user’s cognitive engagement with tasks, and they can be used for cognitive outsourcing. It’s up to the user which choice they make, in ways that will inevitably be determined by the context in which they are making those choices. This means that judgements about language models being intrinsically positive or negative for education don’t really make sense, given that it depends on what users actually do with them in real-world settings. There are wider concerns about the technology and the firms operating it which we need to take seriously: the environmental impact, consolidating the power of big tech firms, the training process involving infringement of intellectual property on an unprecedented scale, the threat of widespread automation and the risks posed to the mental health of young people in particular9. Each of these offers good reason to be critical of the language models and the firms operating them. If we take them all together, there’s a strong political case to be made for urgent regulation of this sector. But whether language models are supportive of education or hostile to it is a question we need to treat more carefully, given the role of user choice in realising these benefits or harms. There’s a risk we set up the issue in a manner which obscures the range of options available to us as institutional actors.

My fear is that those options have significantly narrowed while the sector has remained locked into a reactive mode. But there remain options, and it is important that we recognise them and recover our capacity to act in the face of these challenges. If we find that students are routinely using language models to outsource learning, then we need to ask why rather than simply blaming the student and/or the technology. If students have a transactional relationship with their university, in which they are inclined to seek the maximal outcome with the minimal investment, then we should try and understand why this is. It might be that they’re committed to a nearly impossible quantity of paid work to fund a degree that assumes they have no commitments beyond their studies. It might be they have caring responsibilities or are struggling with a long commute to an institution they can’t afford to live near. It might be that they see their degree as a means to an end, which they approach in a purely instrumental way. It might be they started with the best of intentions but they find the classrooms overcrowded and the teaching lacklustre. It might be some combination of these things, as well as any number of other factors, which lead them to outsource their intellectual work to language models. The point is that we need to know rather than merely assigning culpability to the model.

If we start from the assumption that the model is leading them astray, it suggests that if only we could get rid of the technology, everything would be fine. However, it is not only that we have no way of prohibiting models without imposing a level of surveillance that would be unethical and impractical10. It would also mean refusing to look at the real problems which the influx of language models is revealing within the university system. These technologies are, as philosopher Shannon Vallor (2024: loc 187) puts it, “a mirror of ourselves, not as we ought to be or could be, but as we already are and have long been”11. We owe it to our students, as well as to universities, to look closely at what we see in this mirror. These are not new problems, even if we’re seeing them in a new way. They were always serious problems, even if in some cases language models might be rapidly exacerbating them. The only way to deal with them is to understand the complex interaction between a new technology and a system that was already struggling. This is hard work intellectually, and it’s even harder work organisationally. But unless we commit to this labour, we will be deluding ourselves with comforting stories about a new technology disrupting a previously healthy system.

Neither alien nor superintelligent

This doesn’t mean language models aren’t causing problems in higher education. There are huge problems raised by their widespread uptake by staff and students. But instead of treating language models as “akin to a separate species, a highly autonomous, potentially superintelligent entity”, we can, as the computer scientists Narayanan and Kapoor (2025) put it, treat them as a normal technology. This doesn’t mean denying these are remarkable new technologies as much as insisting that we are still talking about “a tool that we can and should remain in control of” and that “this goal does not require drastic policy interventions or technical breakthroughs”. It is not beyond our capabilities as a sector to develop a strategic response to language models which can cope with the challenges they pose for our existing mode of operation12.

Lessons from earlier technological change

There have been many technological developments that have challenged existing practice within higher education or complicated the assumptions we make about teaching, learning and research. Consider the personal computer, word processor, digitised journal, Wikipedia and social media to name just a few obvious examples. As a millennial academic who started a part-time PhD in 2008, I dimly remember marking my first batch of student assignments by hand. The social platforms which I spent the 2010s studying the impact of within higher education were still in their infancy (Carrigan 2019a). It was still routine to rely on paper journals, such that my PhD supervisor’s bookshelves were as filled with these as with books. This was a comparatively recent experience, and yet these analogue features of academic life have now faded into obscurity such that we are all what Weller (2011) calls digital scholars.

The examples I’m invoking are in many ways smaller than language models. But together they have constituted a continual process of technological change that we have navigated within universities with comparatively little drama, unlike the waves of anxiety and rumination that have accompanied language models. Digital transformation, as Weller (2011: loc 97) puts it, often involves “both a profound change and a continuation of traditional practice”. The pandemic showed how rapidly higher education can change, with a dramatic shift in the mode of delivery for almost all teaching and learning (Nordmann et al 2020). What’s striking about the pandemic is how dramatically it then snapped back to the status quo ante, as digital teaching and learning came to be defined for many academics by the emergency stopgap measures drawn upon during the pandemic (Carrigan et al. 2023). There’s certainly a legacy of the online pivot in academic practice but it’s far smaller than many would have assumed when we were living through these disruptions.

There have been many “surface changes” but “few (if any) instances of genuine disruption of the status quo” while the “seemingly spectacular rises of MOOCs, learning analytics, adaptive testing and personalised learning networks have tended to obscure the continuation of long-standing educational inequalities, inefficiencies and inertias” (Selwyn 2016: loc 2008). This is exactly what we are initially seeing with language models, particularly with regard to how they reflect and amplify existing forms of inequality (Carrigan and Sylvia 2025). Those users who are fluent writers, intellectually confident and enjoy what sociologists call cultural capital13 are literally able to unlock functionality of the language models that other users can’t access. The language model responds to complexity such that what you get out is shaped by what you put into it. If you’re able to put a higher quantity and quality of material into the software, then you’re able to get a higher quantity and quality of material out of it. Furthermore, the rise of Pro subscriptions (e.g. currently £200 for ChatGPT Pro) provides vastly more computational power such that those with the capital can access capabilities which casual subscribers, or non-paying users, simply cannot.

A new digital divide?

Indeed Freeman (2025) found that more privileged students were making more reflective uses of language models. This suggests we might already be seeing what Ragnedda (2017) describes as a third digital divide in which there is a vicious cycle between digital inequality and social inequality. The more privileged are better able to take advantage of digital systems in a way that reinforces and entrenches their privilege. The rise of AI agents capable of working autonomously on tasks for long periods of time will only exacerbate this dynamic. They are by their nature extremely expensive to operate. But a skilled user can deploy what are essentially whole teams of models to perform real-world tasks for them. What happens if we find ourselves in classrooms where some students have teams of agents working proactively for them in the background while other students are reliant on rate-limited access to free models or abstaining entirely from their use? This might sound like science fiction but it is a scenario that we already confront in some classrooms, particularly with taught postgraduate students who are highly technologically literate.

These are real challenges which we need to take seriously. Once we stop talking about ‘artificial intelligence’ it’s easier to describe these problems in specific and actionable ways. As Narayanan and Kapoor (2025) put it, “We do not think that viewing AI as a humanlike intelligence is currently accurate or useful for understanding its societal impacts, nor is it likely to be in our vision of the future”. I would argue the same is true of higher education. Instead of seeing this as a human-like intelligence which we must either adapt to or fight back against, we can focus on how this consumer-facing software with its propensity to support cognitive outsourcing has spread rapidly amongst students and staff without any agreement being reached about appropriate or inappropriate use. The problem is the rapid diffusion of language models without any agreement about how we integrate them into teaching and research, as opposed to the language models themselves being intrinsically destructive. If we reframe the problem in this way, it begins to seem more tractable than if we worry about ‘artificial intelligence’ in all its intimidating vastness.

Mainstream use without shared norms

Freeman’s (2025) research found that 92% of undergraduates at UK universities have used ‘AI tools’ in some form, while 88% have used them in assessment. Once you drill down into this data, you immediately see that use of the software is far from uniform, ranging from using it to explain concepts to producing text which is submitted without editing. I’ll argue in later chapters that we need to grapple with this range if we want to adequately account for how LLMs are being used by students (and staff) rather than just asking whether they are used. It’s striking how little consensus there is amongst students about what is acceptable, with no form of use achieving more than two-thirds agreement on its acceptability (Freeman 2025). It’s not simply that language models have spread rapidly within the sector. The problem is that they’ve become ubiquitous without being normalised. Furthermore, there’s a startling gap between the number of students using language models and those who report having been offered support (36%). Instead of treating LLMs as something which, as Bogost (2025) puts it, “has happened”, many institutions, as well as many academic staff, continue to imagine this as something that “is happening or will happen”. It’s not a coming transformation but rather one which has already occurred at a pace that has left post-pandemic universities struggling to catch up.

If we want to catch up, we have to start by recognising the problem: use of language models is now mainstream amongst staff and students, but there’s a complete lack of agreement about what constitutes appropriate and inappropriate use. The language models themselves do not intrinsically lead to either cognitive enhancement or cognitive outsourcing14. What matters is how they are used by real people in real-world situations. This makes it urgent to create environments in which users approach the language models in thoughtful and reflective ways. If our students and staff are using this software in ways widely regarded as problematic, then we urgently need to understand the conditions driving such use and take responsibility for changing them. Unfortunately, the incentive structures within higher education make that difficult at present. There is the pressure on academic staff to ‘publish or perish’, the many demands on students that aren’t factored into the assumptions made in learning design and the financial pressures which create a real need to improve efficiency by doing familiar tasks more quickly. Language models have hit universities at a profoundly challenging moment which partly explains why the sector has remained caught in a reactive mode.

Establishing an academic culture in which there’s a clear sense of what it means to use language models in careful and responsible ways does not remove these structural challenges. But without such a culture guiding the use (and regulating the abuse) of language models, those structural factors will rapidly lead to this new technology compounding existing problems. It’s a necessary but insufficient condition for coping with language models which we have thus far proved spectacularly unable to meet as a sector. Until the majority of people within an institution can ask “is it ok for me to use a language model in this way?” and receive a clear and coherent answer, the problems we face will only continue to grow. Building this culture doesn’t solve the problems but it is difficult to imagine how we might solve them until such a culture is in place.

A sector under pressure

This is a genuinely difficult situation which has emerged at a point in time when the sector is under intense political and economic stress. The emergence of language models could hardly have come at a worse time in terms of the capacity of universities to cope with them. Failure to adapt to these changes could also be enrolled into existing political attacks upon higher education by those intent on discrediting and dismantling the sector. If the use of language models for cognitive outsourcing becomes embedded, universities’ capacity to produce knowledge (through research) and disseminate knowledge (through teaching and public engagement) will start to erode. It won’t be an immediate process or even a dramatic one because it’s something we’ll only really be able to observe through downstream effects.

However, if universities award degrees to substantial numbers of graduates who lack the fundamental knowledge and skills claimed by their credentials, this will eventually become a matter of urgent political debate. We might not get to the stage of people dying in operating rooms or bridges falling down because doctors and engineers haven’t reliably developed the skills which universities were entrusted to inculcate in them. However remote such outcomes would be, the sector would face devastating political attacks long before professional failures on that scale became visible. It doesn’t need bridges to crumble or for there to be unnecessary deaths on the operating table. A widespread perception that degrees have lost their value would be sufficient. If we cannot get to grips with the cognitive outsourcing enabled by the diffusion of language models, the social role of the university itself is under threat.

The existential threat on the horizon

In countries across the world higher education is under attack from reactionary and authoritarian leaders who see it as an incubator of ‘woke’ values which they have declared the enemy of national unity. Even the Ivy League universities, which many imagined as immune from these pressures, have been through a cataclysm during the first year of the second Trump administration. If Harvard can be brought to heel, with its multibillion-dollar endowment, incomparable alumni network and unrivalled prestige, what hope can other universities really have? A significant decline of trust in degrees will surely be seized upon by strategists within such movements to further discredit the sector. Indeed it’s remarkable that we haven’t yet seen this in any meaningful way. The attack lines write themselves: woke universities relying on woke language models15 to certify vacuous credentials with no real value in the labour market. It’s easy to imagine self-styled centrists providing inadvertent covering fire for such a view, concurring that declining graduate employment means we do in fact need to shrink higher education enrolment. If there’s a sustained attack on the legitimacy of the sector, we risk a fiscal death spiral in which declining budgets intensify the processes which cause the crisis of legitimacy in the first place.

In the next chapter I consider how adapting to AI and integrating AI will both be necessary for navigating our way through this crisis. In the short-term, we need to secure assessments so that we can plausibly assert that credentials actually entail the claimed knowledge and skills. In the medium-term, we need to integrate LLMs into assessments and curricula in ways that develop the skills and subject knowledge that students will need in a transformed technological environment. In the long-term, we need to build institutions that can preserve and sustain their values in radically changing economic and technological circumstances.

The problem is how we sequence these different ambitions because there are fundamental tensions between them, particularly given what I argue in Chapter 3 is the fundamental uncertainty in the economic conditions shaping the development of language models. At some point in the near future we will see either the initial public offerings (IPOs) of the major labs and/or the investment bubble bursting. This will bring about rapid change in the operations of AI labs, their pricing frameworks and the core characteristics of the models themselves. It’s difficult to do anything longer term until this landscape is clear, at least without being locked into a pricing model which cannot last once AI labs begin the long march towards profitability. But it’s also untenable to muddle through with limited mitigation measures, particularly when the quantity of students and staff using language models as part of their daily work continues to rise. It’s an intractable situation that easily lends itself to either strategic inaction, reacting to developments in a piecemeal way, or overaction which risks locking us into a trajectory we might later regret.

A lesson in trust

Early on a Sunday morning in early 2024 I made the mistake of checking my e-mail before I’d had my coffee. What I discovered felt like it would ruin my day: a message from a Boston-based law firm threatening legal action. They claimed I had used a copyrighted image on my blog without permission and demanded immediate restitution to avoid further escalation. It was an unsettling e-mail to receive from what appeared to be a legitimate law firm with a large number of staff and a real presence in the world. However, as I browsed the website and the caffeine hit my system, I rapidly began to realise that something wasn’t quite right about the message. The legal details of the letter appeared to be wrong in a number of ways. There was also something eerily generic about the website that I couldn’t put my finger on. It was only when I searched for the firm and discovered no digital footprint beyond their own website that I realised this was clearly not what it presented itself as being. Once I discovered the domain name had been registered in Iceland the year before, I realised this was obviously a scam. It appeared that chatbots had been used to produce a sufficiently plausible website that it would persuade their targets that the e-mails came from a legitimate law firm.

The e-mail didn’t ruin my day but it did leave me preoccupied by what language models mean for trust. My immediate reaction to the e-mail was to look at the firm’s website. It appeared obviously legitimate to me. Why? It was an extensive site with detailed blurbs and photos of a large and diverse staff. There was even a blog in which those staff opined on a range of legal matters. Even just three years ago it would have been extremely costly to construct such a website in order to deceive an audience. It might have taken a team days to write so much material. Yet now it’s possible for someone to generate the website, text and images in a matter of minutes. What I found chilling about this experience was how lazily it had been put together. It didn’t survive twenty minutes of serious scrutiny but then it wasn’t intended to stand up to investigation. It just needed to persuade a casual viewer that they should take the legal threat seriously.

The proxies we rely on

It was a dramatic illustration of a phenomenon which is often imperceptible. When we make judgments about whether to trust a person or an organisation, we rely on proxies for the characteristics we’re evaluating. We can’t see the legitimacy of the law firm, but we can see features which we associate with legitimate organisations. The capacity of models to generate text, image and video calls into question many of these assumptions. It could once be assumed that a firm with a website, a domain name and profile pictures of staff was a real organisation with real staff. It used to be so time-consuming to produce these outputs that we could safely assume only a real organisation would be able to do so. Yet now it’s something which a fraudster can produce in a few hours of work.

The same is true for individuals. Until recently if we received an e-mail we would assume it had been written by the sender, unless we knew they had an executive assistant. It’s no longer the case that an e-mail means someone wrote it for themselves or delegated the task to someone else. It’s now possible for commercially available models to answer my e-mail for me, if I was inclined to ask them to. This wholesale outsourcing of e-mail is (thankfully) still a fringe phenomenon but it’s easy to see how it might spread, particularly given how widespread the polishing of e-mails using models has become1. Increasingly, this functionality is just one click away in software like Microsoft Outlook. This automation helps individuals save time but there’s a risk it becomes more difficult to trust the people we’re interacting with as this becomes a widespread practice.

The problem is that we can’t usually know if someone has outsourced their communication to a language model. Yet we start looking for markers of artificiality which may or may not be there. Does the message sound a little strange? Is there something robotic about how they’re communicating? They may have outsourced the writing to a language model, but it could equally mean they’ve e-mailed us in a rush. The perfect spelling and grammar might strike us as suspicious. Does anyone really write like that? Or could it just be that they’ve written the e-mail with care?

Suspicion in the seminar room

It’s particularly problematic when it comes to student work. There’s a particular kind of surface fluency that has become a familiar feature of student assignments: a smoothness to grammar and syntax which feels startlingly artificial if you compare it to examples of student work prior to ChatGPT. Many academic staff imagine this means the student must have entirely outsourced their work to a model. However, it could just as easily be work they’ve produced themselves before using a model to polish the final text. The inevitable tendency to speculate can be deeply corrosive because we can rarely confirm if we are correct about our suspicions.

In late 2025 a mature student at Staffordshire University confronted a lecturer about their use of ChatGPT in an online session which was filmed. A clip from the film was then published by The Guardian. He asserted that “I know these slides are AI-generated, I know that everyone in this meeting knows these slides are AI-generated, I would rather you just scrap these slides… I do not want to be taught by GPT”. I wonder if other academics felt as uncomfortable as I did when watching this video. I’m confident I can justify my use of language models but could I justify it in response to an irate student attacking that use in real time? What if the student didn’t accept the justification I offered? What if the interaction was recorded in order to share it online? I found it hard to watch this video and not be immediately consumed by anxiety.

If we doubt our students and colleagues we must recognise they might in turn be doubting us. I’ve banished em-dashes from my writing because I’m so conscious that readers automatically infer the use of models when they see them. There are countless tiny adjustments we might make to pre-empt the judgements we imagine others might be making of us. When you hear a complaint that reviewer two has ‘obviously’ used ChatGPT or that LinkedIn is now filled with ‘AI slop’ you are nudged to consider if your own peer reviews or social posts might be interpreted this way. If you’re not using models you might worry you’re inadvertently writing in ways that might lead others to assume you are. If you are using them you silently worry you are using them in ways colleagues find problematic. But it’s difficult to seek clarification when everyone seems to be so frustrated by the apparent proliferation of model-generated content.

From moral judgement to diagnosis

The crisis of trust means it’s difficult to talk about a challenge which everyone is facing. This technology is so new we still lack a meaningful sense of what constitutes appropriate and inappropriate use. But how could those norms emerge when we remain locked within an edgy individualism that leaves us reluctant to talk about our use in case others criticise it? Meanwhile the mistrust between staff and students continues to grow. Consider how many academic conversations revolve around the perceived presence of model outputs in student work. Why would we not expect students to be having similar conversations about academics using language models in harmful and careless ways? It’s harder for students to call out staff because of the power differentials involved but the Staffordshire example illustrates that it’s not impossible. Indeed, if this atmosphere of mistrust continues to fester, we should expect these callouts to become more frequent and high profile. If we assume the worst of our students, can we really be surprised if they begin to assume the worst of us?

It’s tempting to react to these changes in moralistic terms. It’s easy to slip into the language of ‘cheating’ and ‘malpractice’ to describe students. But there’s been a pervasive lack of guidance provided to students that would help them understand what is and is not acceptable in this landscape. Where this guidance has been provided it tends to be at such an abstract level that it has little capacity to actually support their decision-making. It doesn’t answer the question “is it ok to do this with a chatbot?” but rather provides principles which they are expected to think about when trying to answer this question for themselves. We have placed our students in an impossible situation and then been surprised when they fail to meet the diffuse and contradictory standards we have imposed on them. The underlying reason for this is that the academic community has failed to reach any meaningful agreement about what those standards should be.

There are certainly uses that seem problematic to pretty much everyone, such as submitting entirely model-generated work for an assignment. But even in these cases we urgently need to recover the capacity to respond diagnostically rather than morally. If students are outsourcing their work to models, then what does this tell us about how we’ve organised teaching and learning? Are there specific factors which might be driving this behaviour for particular groups of students? For example the paid work undertaken by many students often significantly outstrips what is assumed in learning design. Or students might have caring responsibilities. They might be grappling with long commutes because they can’t afford to live near the campus. In many cases language models might be seized upon as a way of making an impossible situation more possible, even if it involves malpractice. The point is not to excuse the behaviour but rather to understand the origins of it. This means rejecting the simplistic explanation that models are causing people to cheat. The more honest reading is that they offer a new way of resolving problems which existed long before the technology arrived2.

A multidimensional crisis of trust

The challenge universities are facing isn’t just how widespread this technology has become since November 2022. If there was agreement about what constituted appropriate and inappropriate use for both staff and students, then we could handle this. The problem arises because everyone knows that many other people are using it, but there’s disagreement about which uses are ok and which uses are not ok. Instead of a reflective practice in which we grapple with what this means for both students and staff, we face what I will argue is a multidimensional crisis of trust. The problem is not simply each relationship in itself but the consequences which accumulate when all these relationships are breaking down simultaneously:

  1. Staff don’t trust students. Too many default to the assumption that students will inevitably use models for academic misconduct. Even when this assumption is warranted it’s unhelpfully treated as a moral rather than a sociological phenomenon. Rather than analysing the conditions which might lead a student to malpractice, such as an unsustainable quantity of paid work, the focus immediately turns to how to identify and hold them responsible. There’s an incipient paranoia in which cheating feels like it’s everywhere, but we cannot be sure who is cheating. There is an accurate recognition that student writing has fundamentally shifted but the explanation for this defaults to the most uncharitable one available. There’s little acknowledgement of the range of ways in which students might use models. This makes it difficult to have conversations with teaching staff who might otherwise be a source of guidance to students.
  2. Students don’t trust staff. There is growing awareness of the degree to which staff are using language models to prepare teaching materials. Jisc’s (2024) survey from the 23/24 academic year found nearly a quarter of academics reporting using models in their teaching. We can expect the number has only increased since then. It’s inevitable that students will develop a similar set of ‘red flags’ to those which staff are prone to looking for in student work. For example, it’s easy to see how the em-dash, popular with many academics, might be seen by students as a tell-tale sign of model use. There’s a legitimate accusation of dual standards in which student use of models is intensely policed while staff seemingly use models with impunity. This could also be framed in terms of value for money, with students resenting the use of language models by staff given the escalating costs of their post-pandemic degree.
  3. Students don’t trust students. Once use of models by students is perceived as ubiquitous there’s an inevitable fear of being penalised if you don’t use the models. It risks creating a toxic climate in which students feel they need to use models in order to compete with their peers. My experience has been that suspicions and accusations of model-related misconduct can be tied up in personal disagreements in extremely complex and corrosive ways. The problem is not so much that students suspect other students of using models problematically but rather that these suspicions reflect and exacerbate existing fault lines within the student community.
  4. Staff don’t trust staff. It’s become commonplace to see academics on LinkedIn or Bluesky complain about a peer review which they suspect has been written by a language model. There are collaborations which fail because academics can’t reach an agreement about the use of chatbots in shared work. I’ve found myself reading an article and strongly suspecting it was entirely written by a model, before being taken aback by how insidious that reaction is. There are existing stresses and strains in relationships between staff which easily get tangled up in concerns about the role of models in higher education.

The breaking of the content/producer link

There’s an underlying shift taking place in the relationship between content and producers. When we encounter something which has been produced (whether it’s an e-mail, a student essay or a slide deck) we imagine we can reliably infer something about the person who has produced it. In the case of the student essay we assume we can make inferences about their learning and knowledge by examining the form and content of the essay. When we encounter a slide deck we assume the person who produced it spent time thinking about the best way to present their ideas to us. The reality is these inferences were always more fragile than we tended to assume: the slide deck could have been adopted from a colleague before being ad-libbed and the essay might have been purchased from an essay mill.

What we’re now seeing is a systemic breakdown of the content/producer link such that distrust in a particular item risks becoming a generalised mistrust in our colleagues and students. I worry we might soon see a tipping point, in which the capacity to place trust in the institution as a site of a common endeavour fatally breaks down. The results of this would be catastrophic for the sector. So much in universities relies on trust and goodwill to keep things operating smoothly. If this breakdown takes place we could expect the secondary systems for managing discord (mitigating circumstances, appeals panels, complaints boards etc.) to be rapidly overwhelmed. Trust breaks down slowly at first, then all at once.

When someone attributes model-based malpractice to another person they are effectively accusing them of behaving inauthentically. It means they are not really showing up. They are not playing by the same rules as the rest of us. When that mutates into a sense that no one is playing by the rules we are entering a truly worrying situation. There’s emerging evidence of the penalties and stigma attached to model use which we need to take seriously (Reif et al. 2025). However, this behavioural literature doesn’t take into account the moral feelings which often accompany such accusations: what it feels like to imagine other people cheating, the sense of grievance accompanying this and how this might play out in terms of already fragile working relationships.

Uneven exposure to suspicion

I would feel able to negotiate a student complaint about use of model generated text. I would explain how I use models to improve the presentation of my ideas rather than to generate those ideas in the first place. But I’m a white male middle-class mid-career academic who studies language models and has students who are taking my classes because they want to learn about models from me. It would be far more difficult for many colleagues to negotiate such a conversation, which is why many academics will have watched that Staffordshire video and thought “god I’m glad that wasn’t me”. This is a situation in which many of us might soon find ourselves, regardless of whether we do indeed use models. Universities urgently need to begin preparing for these interactions.

There were always stresses and strains in these relationships. The pandemic and its adaptations strained these relationships further, changing how we relate to each other within higher education. Once we’ve had the experience of work and study being entirely mediated by screens it’s difficult to return to face-to-face interaction as if nothing had changed. How we work together had already undergone a radical shift which changed how we experienced trust in our organisation. It feels different to work with people you mostly, or entirely, see online than it does to work with people you primarily meet face-to-face. In this sense it couldn’t have been a worse time for any major new problem to have emerged within higher education.

This is why the language of ‘artificial intelligence’ is so unhelpful in its framing of an alien element which is suddenly intruding into already strained interpersonal relationships. Language models are weird enough in their capacity to respond fluently to our messages. They feel like intelligent agents even when we know intellectually they are not. Under these circumstances we need to be intensely careful about how we think and talk about the problems we are confronting. There was so much free-floating anxiety already circulating in the sector and the more it attaches itself to the evocative term ‘artificial intelligence’, the more difficult it will be to work through the practical challenges in the sober and careful ways needed. This is particularly crucial because there are two agendas through which those problems are being defined which have tended to talk past each other in sometimes bad tempered and unhelpful ways: adapting to large language models and integrating large language models.

Adapting to AI

The initial response to language models within universities was driven by a need to adapt to their use by students. It was now possible that a piece of writing seemingly produced by a student was in fact produced by a machine. It couldn’t be assumed that the writing which they had submitted in their name was in fact written by them. This was a significant transformation in how students were perceived even if it was not, strictly speaking, a real change. It was always possible that student work was in fact written by someone else. Indeed that was the entire business model for the essay mills which rapidly grew through the affordances of the web in a multi-sided market which united buyers and sellers. What had changed is that it could now feel likely in many instances.

There is something which feels different about the potential role of language models. They feel more intrusive, alien and ubiquitous than essay mills. Even if technology had turbocharged malpractice through online platforms it didn’t feel like it was something new and threatening. In contrast language models are radically new and unsettlingly fluent. It feels as if they are suddenly everywhere, tempting students to cheat through the immediacy of the responses they offer. Unlike the essay mill that required a substantial payment to compel a human expert to produce an assignment, it is now possible for a student to produce instantaneous responses without even paying for a subscription.

This is new. It is eerie. It is a recipe for paranoia. Our encounters with it in higher education took place against the backdrop of endless claims about ‘artificial intelligence’ and how it would transform every aspect of our lives. This primed everyone teaching in universities to examine their students’ work with a newly critical eye, searching for what were imagined to be the telltale signs of machine writing. To assume everyone might be cheating doesn’t produce a working environment that feels safe or validating. The rapidity with which many academics jumped to that conclusion suggests universities, prone to functioning as what Hall (2014) calls anxiety machines, did not feel safe or validating to their staff to begin with. An institution in which most staff could immediately jump to this conclusion was not an institution that was in a healthy place prior to these developments.

The instinct to constrain

The initial instinct for many institutions was to constrain the technology. To look for systems that could detect the presence of machine writing. To move to formats such as oral exams where a student couldn’t rely on the language model to guide them. To return to face-to-face exams where a student had no choice but to produce their own writing. These impulses were in some ways pedagogically regressive, reverting to practices which the sector had moved away from for largely sound reasons. Introducing a reliance on high-stakes assessments, which students had neither expected nor were prepared for, felt obviously problematic.

For a thinker like Pratschke (2024) these mitigations were a failure to grapple with the educational possibilities of the language models. Instead of recognising that something fundamental had shifted, universities were leaning into outdated processes of assessment. They were gearing up for a detection arms race which could only lead to intensified surveillance pedagogy. Instead of adapting to AI such enthusiasts argued that we must focus on integrating AI. This was the point at which those animated by a longer-term vision and those focused on short-term challenges began to talk past each other. While thoughtful integrationists like Pratschke recognised there were pressing challenges which needed to be addressed, they cautioned that without a longer-term vision of technological integration this problem solving would be narrow and reactive.

However, pedagogically regressive doesn’t necessarily mean wrong. After ChatGPT’s launch I felt dismayed by the calls to revert to in person exams because I felt they imagined we could dispel this technology if only we were sufficiently motivated. It was obvious to me this was impossible. If massively hyped software is made freely available to students3 who engage with their studies through a range of personal devices then how are you going to keep it out? If the biggest corporations in the world are committed to spending billions of dollars on pushing a technology it means we’re going to see a lot of it4. This is a depressing statement about the power which tech giants possess in the 2020s but it doesn’t cease to be accurate simply because it’s dispiriting to recognise. I was deeply suspicious in these initial months that mitigations were motivated by a fundamental denial of the situation in which we found ourselves. Instead of engaging with this technology, working out what it can and can’t do, adaptationists seemed fixated on keeping it out of the university instead.

Why securing assessment still matters

I’ve since realised that the motivations being wrong doesn’t mean the associated measures aren’t necessary. As I argued in Chapter 1, there’s a political need to ensure universities are securing assessment in order to fight off growing attacks upon the sector. There’s a real risk we might see a precipitous collapse of trust in credentials sufficient to overturn the current economic model of higher education. If even a small portion of the predicted AI-driven white-collar job losses emerge over the next decade then the status of degrees will be even more contested than it is today. We simply can’t assume that degrees will retain the baseline level of socioeconomic desirability and cultural allure which they have enjoyed for most of our lifetimes.

Once a majority of students are using LLMs as a routine part of their studies, we have to acknowledge the real and justified doubts this engenders about the status of degrees. While many students use models in thoughtful and responsible ways, the evident fact of their widespread use means we can’t assume a credential necessarily reflects the learning which the institution claims for it. The activity involved in achieving the qualification has potentially changed so much that the fundamental assumptions made about the qualifications are inevitably revisited. It’s essential not only to address these doubts but also to be seen to be addressing them if we want to forestall a significant erosion of trust in higher education. There is the problem itself but also the perception of the problem by external stakeholders.

Even if external stakeholders somehow failed to notice these developments, it’s still something we are required to address for the simple fact that we want to ensure our students are actually learning. The brutal fact underwriting the need to adapt to AI is that we have no meaningful capacity to prohibit our students from using this technology. The diffusion has been so rapid that we are now facing a change which has already happened, even while most institutions remain preoccupied by a transformation they imagine as impending (Bogost 2025). Adapting to AI is necessary if universities want to secure their public legitimacy in the longer term. The problem is that integrating AI is also necessary if we want to preserve the relevance of what we do.

Integrating AI

Over the last few years I’ve occasionally encountered a hostility to my research which I’ve never experienced before. What’s curious about this is that I’ve sometimes been accused of being overly critical and on other occasions I’ve been told I’m insufficiently critical. Indeed on a recent panel an anonymous commentator suggested I must have been taking money from Microsoft, presumably because that was the only explanation they saw for why I wasn’t simply condemning the technology and the firms behind it. I’m not highlighting this to pat myself on the back (though part of me does think I’m doing something right if I’m annoying both sides of a divide) but rather to point to how polarised the debate around ‘artificial intelligence’ has become. I don’t think these critics are wrong to draw attention to the power of big tech firms in higher education. Indeed I’ve spent years researching what I and others term the platform university precisely for this reason5.

We should all be concerned about higher education’s dependence on corporate platforms. Komljenovic et al. (2025: 7) draw attention to the novel dependencies “created by outsourcing capacity development and future planning to private actors” as well as the downstream consequences for teaching, research and public engagement. Investment in (external) platforms becomes the key means through which the university expands its capabilities within core domains of operation. Technology platforms enable universities to perform important tasks but at the cost of locking in dependence upon external suppliers. For example, it’s possible for a university to switch between Learning Management Systems (LMS) if they are willing to bear the costs of doing so. But switching away from the LMS altogether is a more prohibitive undertaking once the organisation has restructured itself around their affordances. This highlights how concerns about dependence need to include not just particular providers but rather entire categories of technology. These are dominated by private providers who are adept at influencing procurement processes, exploiting risk aversion and institutional inertia to ensure open-source alternatives don’t feel viable for university leaders.

It’s easy to cast this in moralistic terms such that you are either for or against technology. There’s certainly an element of this at work in our present debate about language models. I’ve written in the past about the cultural politics of digital technology: how our discussions of technology get pulled towards the utopian and dystopian narratives that litter the cultural landscape (Carrigan 2025: ch 6). When it comes to higher education though, I think something deeper than this is taking place. It’s not just a conflict between competing orientations towards the technology, one boosterish and the other critical, but rather between two strategic logics which roughly correlate to those positions but aren’t reducible to them:

Two strategic logics

  1. Adapting to AI: The rapid uptake of models by students6 creates practical challenges in areas such as assessment integrity, academic writing, student engagement and graduate employability. It is a fact on the ground to which we must respond.
  2. Integrating AI: The innovation made possible by language models creates opportunities for what we do within universities to be transformed, particularly with regards to teaching and learning. It is an emerging condition which we must actively shape.

It’s remarkably easy for advocates of these logics to talk past each other. If you’re preoccupied with adapting to AI, you’re primarily concerned with what’s happening now. If you’re preoccupied with integrating AI, you’re looking towards where we should be going. It would be too simplistic to say the former is present-tense and the latter is future-tense. Adapting to AI means avoiding a future in which these problems get worse, while integrating AI means using present opportunities to realise a desirable future. But they nonetheless entail a different emphasis that creates the risk of approaching the other logic in a slightly baffled way. Why can’t they see how many problems this technology is causing for learners? Why can’t they see that we have to learn to live with this technology? They’re legitimate questions which are far too easily dismissed by the other side in what feels like an increasingly bad-tempered debate. The fact that so much of this crucial professional dialogue is unfolding over social media certainly doesn’t help matters.

Critics of language models are often responding to integration agendas. They see these as dangerous attempts to embed commercial software so tightly into the fabric of the university that we might never remove it. For advocates of AI integration, the political economy seems like a distraction from the core challenge of using this remarkable new technology to enact an overdue transformation of teaching and learning. They can recognise the flaws of actually existing AI labs while still arguing we need to embrace the technology we have. What matters is taking advantage of these new opportunities. There’s an opportunity for richer, more engaging and more personalised forms of learning which the affordances of the technology make possible. This is a serious pedagogical proposition which I engage with at length in the final chapter, even if I remain cautious about weaker versions of the argument which focus on ‘AI skills’.

The workplace argument

A common claim in recent years has been that students will be using models in their workplaces, so we are failing them if we expel the technology from the university system. There’s a naivety to this claim which must be (gently) called out: it assumes current models will develop inexorably over time in exactly the manner AI labs claim. It imagines future workplaces where today’s students will be using what are essentially just more powerful versions of ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini. This has often been framed in terms of ‘prompt engineering’, which is claimed to be the skill of the future that all students will need. This fails to recognise how models are already changing, such that what Mollick (2024a) called a co-intelligence we need to learn to work with has instead become a wizard that acts on our behalf. It’s simply not necessary for most users to prompt carefully in the way they did with earlier models.

In the case of inline automation software such as Copilot 365 and Grammarly, models proactively nudge users into offloading the work of articulation. If you can click a button to “rewrite” or “make longer” without needing to explain what or how you want this to happen, a threshold is crossed in which the substance of the task is being defined by the model rather than the user. There is a worrying trajectory in which the design of consumer-facing software appears to be reducing the need for the user’s thoughtful engagement. This is understandable in the sense that it produces software which is easier to use and more liable to produce productivity gains. But it is a substantial challenge to higher education where the cognitive friction is exactly where the most important work tends to take place.

The notion we must train our students to ‘use AI’ (largely construed in terms of ‘prompting skills’) not only interprets the development of the models in a misleading way, it’s also naive about workplaces themselves. Organisations will be changed radically by the continued development and diffusion of this technology, such that jobs will be changed and jobs will be lost en masse. Anyone who claims in 2026 to be able to define an ‘AI skills’ curriculum which is future-proof is simply not recognising the complexity of what’s coming. However, employers are clearly reporting that they desire ‘AI skills’ in graduates even if attempts to define these are unpersuasive. There are core concepts that can guide us, such as AI literacy7 and ‘21st century skills’ (e.g. creativity, reflection and curiosity) which clearly connect to the affordances of models, even as they continually change. My point is not that we shouldn’t be trying to articulate an AI employability agenda in higher education. It’s rather that developing such an agenda is bewilderingly complex, given the intersection of socioeconomic and technological change, in a manner which too many people working in this space are reluctant to acknowledge.

Credentials, careers and ‘AI skills’

How students perceive credentials could change significantly over the coming years. There is growing evidence of a reduction in entry-level recruitment, even if the causal link between job numbers and the rollout of LLMs remains unclear. If this becomes a sustained trend, we could see a rapid increase in demand for skills imagined to enhance competitiveness in the labour market, even where their actual value is more ambiguous. If ‘AI skills’ come to be seen as a route to employability, that perception will matter, regardless of whether the payoff matches the rhetoric. The credential inflation which has driven the late-stage massification of higher education is itself animated by a diffuse unease about the future: a sense of needing to secure whatever competitive advantage is possible in order to reach a point of relative safety on a sinking ship. If this dynamic intensifies in response to declining employment, particularly where such decline is perceived to result from language models, it has significant implications for higher education.

In this sense, we do need to take the aspiration for ‘AI skills’ seriously, and we should expect this demand to grow over time. This is likely to generate pressure from students for forms of AI integration that extend well beyond the limited purview of mitigation measures. It implies embedding LLMs within curricula, teaching with and about them in context-sensitive and discipline-specific ways, rather than relying on generic provision such as university-wide courses in ‘prompt engineering’ or ‘AI literacy’. This is a complex undertaking with significant demands on staff time and disciplinary expertise. The challenge is not only resourcing this work and keeping provision up to date but ensuring that sufficient knowledge exists within departments in the first place. There is a capacity problem for AI integration which requires significant investment in staff training and development. This is particularly difficult in a rapidly evolving technological landscape where both the capabilities of the models and their institutional significance remain in flux.

Teaching with and about language models

There is no reason why teaching with and about LLMs must involve advocacy of their use. If graduates are encountering a labour market transformed by models, living in a world where the data centre build-out is exacerbating the climate crisis, critical AI scholarship which helps them understand those societal and environmental implications will be imperative. Integration should not be understood as synonymous with endorsement. Unless integration makes space for critical evaluation and limitations on use, then it will not be adequate to the needs and interests of our students. The point is not to abandon critique in order to build ‘AI’ into the fabric of the university; it is rather to ensure critique effectively serves our students and supports them as citizens in the world they are encountering. There is no reason for us to see this as in tension with teaching the practical capabilities which students perceive to be valued in the labour market. The point is that students need both practical capabilities and critical insights to be ready for the world they are graduating into. Indeed, one could plausibly expect that knowing when not to use LLMs, as well as being able to work effectively without them, will be an exceptionally valuable skill in the near future.

The Dilemma Facing Universities

The crisis facing universities requires both adaptation and integration. If we don’t adapt to the diffusion of models, we risk a collapse in the perceived value of degrees. The current system cannot continue unchanged once we have a majority of students routinely using chatbots throughout their studies. It simply upends too many of the assumptions underlying established practices of assessment and conceptions of integrity. If left untended, the problem doesn’t go away. It festers as the everyday life of the university begins to be filled with mundane instances of staff and students suspecting each other of problematic use of models. These implications for trust are potentially existential for the sector, to the extent they erode the legitimacy which underwrites the current funding model. This could be rapidly exacerbated if the crisis is seized upon by external actors pushing towards a defunding of the sector.

If we don’t work towards the integration of models we risk a crisis of relevance. While it’s profoundly difficult to define an ‘AI skills’ agenda that won’t rapidly become obsolete, there is a clear need for teaching methods and curricula to remain in contact with the technological conditions in which graduates will live and work. This doesn’t mean a narrow embrace of technical training, which would itself be a recipe for rapid obsolescence, but rather a more expansive concern with how to live well with technology in a changing world. If we cannot do this, what we offer students will become increasingly unmoored from the practical circumstances they will encounter beyond the university. This is a recipe for irrelevance which could prove as existential for higher education as the crisis of trust, albeit on a longer timescale.

Why each agenda undercuts the other

The problem for universities isn’t that both adaptation and integration are required. The problem is that each strategic logic pushes in a different direction, yet neither can be dispensed with. The mitigation measures involved in adapting to AI take us further away from meaningful integration. How can we enable conversations about how to use LLMs creatively while much of the institution’s energy is devoted to securing large swathes of activity against their influence? Even partial and targeted attempts at prohibition create a situation in which staff and students feel unable to talk about their use. If there’s a sense that use is at least potentially wrong, with no one sure about which practices fall within acceptable boundaries, it becomes extremely difficult for reflective practice to emerge in the manner required for AI integration.

Evidence suggests that teaching staff already find it challenging to have conversations with students about models because they feel beyond their range of expertise in dealing with this technology (Kahn et al. 2025). If an atmosphere of mistrust reigns within the university, these conversations become exceptionally difficult. Yet without them, it is hard to see how AI integration, actively making these systems part of teaching and learning in practice, can take place. Language models may become an object of study within the curriculum, but their use as a learning method and their integration into assessment are substantially hindered. This fundamentally constrains the kind of learning that can occur, as AI becomes something discussed in the abstract rather than engaged with in practice.

Any move towards meaningful AI integration will in turn undercut attempts to adapt to AI. The most ambitious initiatives involve institutional procurement decisions, securing university-wide access to enterprise platforms such as ChatGPT Edu or Microsoft Copilot 365. In doing so, the university establishes a baseline level of access for staff and students, ensuring that everyone can draw on a frontier model within teaching and learning. The problem is that such a move is easily read as a sweeping legitimation of whatever use can be made of these tools. In some cases this simply formalises practices that are already widespread. However, where this introduces new functionality, as with the frictionless automation Copilot embeds within Microsoft Office, it becomes more problematic. How can a university plausibly ask students not to cognitively outsource their thinking when the word processor it provides proactively invites them to do exactly that each time they open a new document? The wave of student and staff protests surrounding early enterprise deals in American universities illustrates the tensions inherent in such moves.

There are significant opportunities offered by AI integration which could transform universities in positive ways. The problem is not the direction of travel but how that change is embedded. A top-down rollout in which the provision of technology precedes the cultural transformation necessary to incorporate it into teaching, research and public engagement risks accelerating use without clarifying its meaning. By its nature this cannot be a rapid process, at least if there is to be a meaningful expectation that these systems will be used in careful and responsible ways. It requires experimentation, space for staff to try things out in relatively safe environments and time for shared understandings to emerge before practices are endorsed and stabilised within routine work. These imperatives sit uneasily with the rule-making and prohibitions that adapting to AI necessitates. In some cases they directly undercut them, licensing experimentation with one hand while seeking to curtail it with the other.

Looking beyond the university

These strategic logics are not inherently incompatible. Under certain conditions, we could move in an orderly way from adapting to AI in the short term, through piecemeal integration in the medium term, towards a more expansive rethinking of the university as the technologies it uses change. The point, however, is that both logics are necessary. This helps explain why advocates of each position so often talk past one another: they are each right in their own terms. The problem is how to pursue them in a coherent sequence, and under what conditions this becomes possible. What I have described as the dilemma is precisely this: the tendency of integrating AI to undercut the conditions for adapting to it, and vice versa.

However, this analysis has so far focused on conditions internal to the university. We also need to situate these dilemmas within the broader macroeconomic context in which AI has acquired such urgency across organisations. It is this context which makes institutional decisions in the near future so fraught, but also where the possibility of a way forward can be found. The likely bursting of the AI bubble renders short-term integration profoundly risky, to the extent that it should be approached with extreme caution. In the next chapter, I explore this political economy in order to clarify these risks, before the final chapter outlines a path forward, strategic under-integration, through which these logics might be coupled together under conditions of profound uncertainty.

“Everything is changing so fast”

In almost every conversation about language models in higher education, there comes a point where someone remarks that “everything is changing so fast”. It has become what one says in order to signal familiarity with the topic. The observation is not wrong, but it is banal. It is certainly true that the technology is developing at pace. It is easy to forget that OpenAI launched ChatGPT only a few years ago. In that time, model capabilities have accumulated in ways that would have seemed implausible at the outset. Systems can now produce detailed reports drawing on vast numbers of sources, generate working software from natural language prompts and create increasingly realistic synthetic media. There has been a great deal of change and there is little indication that it is slowing down.

In my experience, however, this observation has a curious effect. Rather than prompting closer attention to these developments, it often leads to a retreat into what is already familiar. Not only is it a platitude, it can make it harder to think clearly about what is changing and why it matters. The fact that many things are changing does not mean we are unable to make meaningful claims about what may come next. Even if the precise outcomes remain uncertain, there is much about the current landscape that is well understood and can guide decision-making in the sector. To do so, however, we need to examine these changes closely rather than simply affirm them and move on.

The problem is particularly pronounced within a sector where organisational processes often run at a glacial pace. Universities are too slow to cope with these developments, as can be seen so clearly in the failure to adapt to AI explored in the previous chapter. It feels difficult to plan given such rapid development because it is unclear exactly where we will find ourselves in the near future, let alone five or ten years down the line. The problem is that this observation can easily excuse inaction in the face of what feels like an intractable problem. How could we possibly keep up with these developments? Is it even reasonable to expect universities to keep up? There’s a counsel of despair lurking just beneath the surface in many of these discussions, occasionally leaking out within otherwise sober discussions of organisational strategy.

It feels like a race we couldn’t possibly win given how slow our sector is and how fast the technology is developing. A range of seemingly spectacular technologies, such as MOOCs and personalised learning, were heralded as disruptive only to fade into the periphery. There clearly are changes, but these have often left deeper questions about what we do and how we do it entirely untouched (Selwyn 2016: loc 2008). Isn’t it possible that language models could be another example where the initial hype fails to give way to real transformation? It’s a tempting thought which can coexist with a lack of confidence in our ability to cope with the scale of the claimed transformation. There’s no way we could keep up with these developments and perhaps it will turn out it wasn’t even necessary to do this in the first place.

If I’m right, these attitudes can coexist, such that sectoral despair can be reinforced by a creeping suspicion that these challenges might eventually fade away; we should examine the assumptions being made about the future here. I’ve been struck by how frequently the impending trajectory of language models is imagined as a linear trajectory of ever accelerating change. It’s assumed the technology will continue to advance indefinitely at a similar pace to that we’ve seen in the initial years since the launch of ChatGPT. The rhetoric of ‘artificial intelligence’ clearly plays a significant role by subsuming a specific form of technology1 offered by a number of corporations on a for-profit basis into grandiose pronouncements about a technological revolution which will either transform human civilisation or destroy it.

A normal technology with real limits

If we instead treat language models as normal technologies, in the manner advocated by Narayanan and Kapoor (2025), the problems we encounter are far more mundane. There have been remarkable advances in a short space of time, but there remains a fundamental brittleness to language models which even thoughtful enthusiasts like Mollick (2024a) recognise. Many critics within the wider AI field plausibly argue there are fundamental limitations which cannot simply be overcome through ‘scaling laws’, in spite of the breathlessly invoked ‘artificial general intelligence’. Language models are remarkable innovations which have radical implications for what we do within universities. But there are lots of reasons to be sceptical of the assumption that technology will keep improving until we reach the point where we’ve built a machine god that will transform every aspect of human civilisation.

This doesn’t mean we should assume progress will stall. How could it, when the most powerful corporations in the world are investing hundreds of billions of dollars into these developments? It does mean, however, that we shouldn’t expect a continually escalating level of performance from these models as they ascend towards superintelligence. As someone who obsessively tests every major model, as well as using them extensively throughout my working life2, there has been a clear improvement in how useful they are. They’ve also become ever more pleasing to work with, at least in my experience. But the wow moment which I experienced when Claude Opus 3 was released, when I suddenly discovered the language model could talk introspectively about social theory at a level comparable to the actual social theorists I know, hasn’t been matched since then3. We can nonetheless expect functionality to expand as models are built into the software and platforms through which we already organise our lives and conduct our business. There will be innovations as AI labs instruct their developers to search for attention-grabbing features that will give them a commercial advantage in a frantic race to dominate the global market. There are now a range of pathways through which model capabilities are growing in contrast to the fairly predictable increase in performance associated with increasing compute and training data.

Could the models get worse?

Indeed, while Mollick (2024a: loc 993) has provocatively advised that we should “[a]ssume this is the worst AI you will ever use”, there are in fact good reasons to expect language models might actually degrade over the coming years. Far from being the worst language models we’ll ever use, the current generation might in fact be the best, at least in terms of the mass market products which most users are presented with. Once we stop talking about ‘artificial intelligence’ and start talking about language models as commercial software, it’s easier to remember these are firms confronting market conditions which determine their behaviour. The rapid development of models we saw from late 2022 onwards depended on the concentration of compute, training data and capital built up over the previous ten years of what Srnicek (2016) calls platform capitalism. If there wasn’t already a global network of data centres, vast amounts of funding available for research and huge pools of training data which had emerged from social media, language models in their current form simply wouldn’t have been possible (Srnicek 2025).

These political and economic conditions made it possible for AI labs to grow rapidly, but they are unlikely to persist in their current form. There is one point on which almost everyone analysing these developments appears to agree: the current phase of the investment cycle cannot last. It is uncertain when this shift will occur or what all the consequences will be, but it represents an inflection point likely to transform the commercial foundations of the sector. The aim here is not to predict a specific outcome, but to delineate a range of plausible trajectories in order to support strategic thinking. The risk is that universities make long-term decisions based on commercial conditions which are imminently entering into a state of profound uncertainty. The sector needs to tread very carefully in the assumptions it is making about the economic terms under which language models are made available as commercial products.

The Bubble Will Burst

A few days before ChatGPT’s third anniversary, the Financial Times’s City Editor Bryce Elder (2025) joked in a column that “OpenAI is a money pit with a website on top”. The point he was making was deadly serious, pointing to the seemingly unsustainable character of the leading AI lab’s dealmaking. He reported on HSBC research estimating OpenAI’s data centre rental bill as heading towards $620 billion per year, as well as exploring how revenue growth would have to increase to meet those costs. ChatGPT’s reported user base of 1 billion monthly active users4 represents an incredible rate of growth for a product which is only three years old. If we compare that to social media firms, only the growth of TikTok matches. It took other major platforms more like a decade to reach that milestone. For a tech establishment committed to the notion of ‘blitzscaling’, as LinkedIn cofounder Reid Hoffman (2018) puts it, OpenAI looks like an incredible success story. Indeed, it’s difficult to deny how rapidly the language model has spread, both in terms of how many people are using it and how visible it is within wider society.

It would be a mistake for OpenAI to rest on its laurels because, as Elder reports, HSBC’s analysis suggested that even if user numbers reached 3 billion by 2030, the AI lab would still be required to subsidise its users in order to meet costs. That rate of use would leave ChatGPT as ubiquitous as WhatsApp, yet it would still leave annual revenue at only $213.59 billion by 2030. The same research estimated that OpenAI’s cumulative rental costs would be $1.4 trillion by 2033. Even in the reasonable best-case scenario the analysis suggests OpenAI’s business is nowhere near profitability. Not least because the conversion rate for paying subscribers is so low. Only an estimated 5% of users currently pay for the language model, and HSBC’s projections suggest 10% of users will be paying subscribers by 2030. This would leave the vast majority of an ever-expanding user base effectively being subsidised in their use of a costly service. As Doctorow (2025b) observes, this means the unit economics of language models are fundamentally different to those of social media firms. Whereas social media firms became steadily more profitable as they scaled towards global dominance, the current model for consumer-facing language models means they actually become more expensive as they grow.

This suggests that training and operating the language models is sufficiently costly that it simply might not be viable as a subscription-based enterprise. This explains OpenAI’s experiments with social media, browsers, wearable computing, advertising, shopping and software integration. The dizzying range of initiatives doesn’t suggest a firm with a clear path towards profitability as much as an incumbent trying to capitalise on its position by throwing many things at the wall to see what sticks. Their enterprise business appears to be struggling, at least relative to Anthropic, which is pursuing a more focused strategy on enterprise computing and development. This doesn’t mean the business will necessarily fail, even if they don’t reach the projections formulated by the HSBC team in what was essentially a stress-testing exercise. But it does mean that, as Elder (2025) puts it, “each new OpenAI fundraise will be for shovelling cash to data centre owners”. It’s clear the firm will have a continual need for new capital, particularly with the competitive dynamics that are leading AI labs towards ever more rapid iteration of models.

Will investors keep paying?

This raises an obvious question: will investors continue to fund this business? The rhetoric of ‘artificial intelligence’ remains crucial because it indexes what are essentially software products and platform services, with uncertain and precarious business fundamentals, to a revolutionary change imagined just over the horizon. A large number of influential figures in this field believe that machine intelligence could rapidly surpass human abilities in a cycle of self-improvement that escapes our capacity to control it. There’s a cultish element to some of the thinking about artificial general intelligence (AGI) circulating amongst digital elites, with leading research scientists talking casually about the bunkers they will escape to and leading investors possessing well-stocked hideaways for when the apocalypse comes (Becker 2025).

However, there’s also a coldly rational calculation lurking behind the apparently messianic thinking. Google’s former CEO Eric Schmidt argues that the company which achieves AGI will have a value significantly “larger than any other company in history, forever, probably” (Thornhill 2025). The DeepMind founder Demis Hassabis reportedly maintains that, once AGI has been achieved, “the concept of economics would become antiquated, and DeepMind and Google wouldn’t have to worry about making money” (Olson 2024: loc 1168). What if they are right? Even if you think they are almost certainly wrong, it’s not, strictly speaking, irrational for investors to take the prospect of AGI seriously. The consequences for civilisation would be so vast that it’s a possibility which must be seriously weighed if we do not feel able to definitively rule it out.

I’m confident they are wrong. I believe there are clear sociological explanations for what appears to be a kind of groupthink that has taken hold amongst digital elites, all of whom have a vested interest in keeping the hype growing. But am I certain they’re wrong? Of course I’m not. The difference between being a true believer in AGI and refusing to rule it out with certainty is one which too often gets lost in these debates. What might seem like irrational behaviour becomes much more explicable if you imagine others in the room simply asking “what if they are right?” and refusing to rule it out. I suspect there are far fewer signed-up believers in the coming machine god than some AI critics are prone to assuming.

This impulse is licensed by an investment culture geared towards the elusive 10x investments in which a small number of long shots pay off while the majority of investments fail (Mallaby 2022). From this perspective, AGI appears like an investment opportunity that could dwarf the returns offered by Google, Facebook, PayPal et al. to an earlier round of investors. Furthermore, as Srnicek (2025) points out, it’s possible the data centre infrastructure could be repurposed in the event of the AI labs failing to achieve their goals5. This is why we need to distinguish between the prospects for OpenAI and a firm like Alphabet, with their Gemini model existing alongside existing cloud computing, hardware and digital advertising businesses. The AI labs whose existence is tethered to the fortunes of a language model confront a precarity which firms like Alphabet and Microsoft are insulated from due to their otherwise robust finances. If you consider the investment in its own terms, it becomes clear this isn’t irrational for either the AI labs, big tech firms or the venture capitalists6.

What kind of bubble?

The fact it isn’t irrational doesn’t mean it’s not a mistake. There’s widespread concern that the current wave of investment is an economic bubble. The disagreement concerns what kind of bubble this is, how it will pop and what will be left over afterwards. The spectre of the dot-com bubble haunts the present wave of investment: a cautionary tale about how firms can be valued in ways completely unmoored from the fundamentals of their businesses. However, it’s untenable to dismiss OpenAI or Anthropic as the Pets.com of the 2020s. Unlike late 90s startups which could raise money with little more than a dot-com domain7, the AI labs have hundreds of millions of users, revenue in the billions and genuinely innovative products. The dot-com parallel could be more appropriate to the wider network of startups which have little or no viable product at present. Though these could be seen as bets on their human capital, exemplified by former OpenAI staff who were able to leverage their past seniority into immediate multi-billion dollar valuations. Their intention is not to reach the market with a successful product but rather to be ‘acqui-hired’ by the major players locked into an increasingly bitter war for engineering talent.

The political scientist David Karpf (2025) points to three different stories which are told about this last crash. Firstly, there is the narrative about overvalued startups like Pets.com which, as we’ve seen, doesn’t fit the AI labs. Secondly, there is the telecoms story in which an industry was awash with capital from investors recognising communications infrastructure would be key to the internet’s growth. When these firms failed, they sold off bandwidth at huge discounts, leaving a legacy which was crucial to the next phase of digital transformation. Thirdly, there is the Enron story in which what was essentially accounting fraud was presented as corporate innovation overturning existing assumptions and methodologies. It’s this latter story that has drawn attention to the Byzantine deals which link chip manufacturers, cloud computing providers and AI labs into opaque networks of commercial co-dependence. In the Great Recession, the impossibility of accurately evaluating assets circulating across the books of the major banks precipitated a global economic collapse. Tech giants were already being described as similarly ‘too big to fail’ by the former FT journalist Rana Foroohar (2019). The risks have only increased since then, with OpenAI briefly floating the possibility that the US government would need to provide a backstop in the event of a market correction, before rapidly retreating in the face of political backlash.

It matters what kind of bubble this is because different kinds of bubbles leave different kinds of things behind. There are AI critics who would insist we shouldn’t discount the first scenario because, as Ed Zitron (2025) puts it, OpenAI is a firm “completely incapable of making a truly usable, meaningful product”8. But if we accept that AI labs are producing economic value, even if it falls short of what’s required for profitability, there are two scenarios which we need to explore: a concentration of AI labs and a systemic economic crisis:

  1. Concentration of AI labs: We might see a market correction which wipes out many firms while leaving the major players intact. This would increase the concentration of the sector through the remaining AI labs acquiring the human capital and intellectual property of the firms which failed. The AI labs which survived the crash would then enjoy an even more dominant position in the future. It probably wouldn’t be a good thing for society to see further concentration of power in the largest tech firms, e.g. if OpenAI was folded into Microsoft or Anthropic was acquired by Apple. However, this would be unlikely to slow the development of language models in the long term, since the remaining firms would be even better equipped to provide the massive resources needed to train and operate the models.
  2. Systemic economic crisis: The dizzying range of interdependencies between the chip manufacturers, AI labs and big tech firms creates a real prospect of a systemic crisis. Big tech firms have invested across the range of AI labs9 and these investments are paid for in part through cloud computing credits, which the labs then ‘spend’ with the firm which is notionally the investor. Furthermore, the data centre build-out unites all these firms together through an opaque and precarious circular finance predicated on expectations of long-term growth. This means that a crash might not only impact the core firms but ripple through the entire economy because of how central this interdependent cluster of corporations has become to American economic growth. The stakes are raised even further by the geopolitical significance of the chip industry in the struggle for global dominance between China and the United States.

Even OpenAI’s Sam Altman has acknowledged this is a bubble. He observed that “[w]hen bubbles happen, smart people get overexcited about a kernel of truth” before going on to acknowledge that we are “in a phase where investors as a whole are overexcited about AI”10. He suggests we can recognise this economic fact without denying the significance of language models. He claims they are “the most important thing to happen in a very long time”. In this sense, Altman implicitly endorses the first scenario: a crash might be coming, but OpenAI expects to survive it and subsequently thrive.

However, if there’s a systemic crisis, it leaves OpenAI with a far more uncertain future. It’s possible language models as we currently understand them wouldn’t survive this if the core firms collapse and there’s neither the incentive nor the capability to reinflate the financing model. Investors might simply be unwilling to subsidise the costs of mass use of language models post-crash because no one believes any longer there’s a profitable future just over the horizon. While their costs of operation could plausibly make language models unviable as a mass market consumer-facing product, it’s less obvious that enterprise software would share this fate. If language models survive a systemic crash, it would likely be as enterprise software deployed for job replacement: the software would have to pay for itself by reducing headcount. This would involve the reorganisation of existing roles, conceived of as a bundle of tasks, with some being susceptible to automation whereas others are not. The concern expressed by many white-collar workers and trade unions that AI pilots effectively ask them to train their replacements might be well-founded, even if the replacement doesn’t come until after the crash.

Two inflection points

A lack of clarity about what comes next needn’t leave us treating the future as wholly unpredictable. I’ve identified one inflection point: the bubble bursting leading to either a concentration of the AI market or a wider economic crisis. The other inflection point comes if OpenAI and Anthropic reach public markets after their IPO preparations began in late 2025. As we saw earlier, the post-IPO trajectory of pets.com came to symbolise the dot-com bubble bursting, suggesting how these inflection points might run together in practice. But if we assume the IPO for one or both of the labs is successful, it creates a familiar dynamic in which increased investor oversight and reporting expectations place the business fundamentals under newfound scrutiny. Once public, unprofitable businesses rapidly find themselves under significant pressure to ensure profitability, in the medium term, if not necessarily the short term. These are two plausible inflection points and both lead to the same destination. We urgently need to grapple with the prospect of what the internet critic Cory Doctorow (2025a) calls enshittification if we are to make sound longer-term decisions about investment in language models within higher education.

The Risk of Enshittification

The author and activist Cory Doctorow’s notion of enshittification, describing the deterioration of once enjoyable and valued online services, has become ubiquitous in technology journalism and academic tech criticism. Originally introduced in an essay on his personal blog, it was then reproduced in the Financial Times and has since become a convenient term to refer to a shift in online experience which is widely acknowledged. It has often been used to simply refer to things getting worse but, as Doctorow (2025a: loc 90) points out, the value of the analysis lies in how it “explains the way an online service gets worse, how that worsening unfolds, and the contagion that’s causing everything to get worse, all at once”. It’s a catchy term which names a widely shared experience in which search engines, social platforms and online markets have deteriorated in what they offer to the end user. It suggests a common logic in which “platforms are good to their users” before “they abuse their users to make things better for their business customers” until they ultimately “abuse those business customers to claw back all the value for themselves” (Doctorow 2025a: loc 153).

This is a simple but powerful argument about the commercial logic of platform firms which rely on bringing new users to the platform to offer value to their current users. What economists call network effects highlight how the practical utility of platforms rests on the interactions they make possible; e.g. a messaging platform only you use, or a delivery platform without any vendors has no value. The nature of venture capital means investors are willing to tolerate loss-making enterprises if they are persuaded that they have the potential to eventually dominate a market. This requires telling a story about user growth in which current trends plausibly gesture towards long-term market dominance. It’s in the interests of the platform to subsidise the experience at this stage, running at a loss in order to maximise its ability to attract new users. Investors will tolerate this because of the prospect of longer-term market dominance and the profitability which comes with it. There is a winner-takes-most logic to network effects because the platform with the widest user base tends to be the most useful to end users.

From subsidy to extraction

The right-wing tech investor Peter Thiel (2014) famously argued that tech firms can and should trend towards monopoly because this facilitates greater innovation due to economies of scale. What Thiel rarely explores publicly is how this (near) monopoly power can be exploited once the initial subsidy is withdrawn. Once a significant user base has been accumulated, platforms can rely on lock-in effects as a consequence of the costs of switching between them (Doctorow 2025a). For example, the network we develop on one social platform cannot be easily moved to another platform. The subscription fee users pay to Amazon incentivises them to always use the service rather than pay delivery fees elsewhere. Platforms are adept at imposing switching costs which can eventually be exploited in order to claw back some of the value initially lost through subsidy during the growth stage.

Once users are locked in, it’s no longer necessary to cultivate their loyalty, particularly when there are business customers to be prioritised over consumers. The promises made about privacy can be disregarded to sell ever more sophisticated advertising products. The search facilities which once allowed users to find exactly the product they were looking for can be displaced by firms paying to ensure their items are placed in the results. It was once good to be a consumer on the platform. It then becomes good to be a business on the platform, paying for access to those consumers. Until the enshittification cycle reaches the point where the business customers too find themselves exploited. This is the point at which the platform exploits its dominance in order to begin clawing back all the value in the digital fiefdom it now securely occupies. Examples of this range from the deliberate sabotaging of Google search results through to Amazon prioritising advertised products over organic search results and Twitter/X gutting content moderation and user safety in order to deliver an ideologically transformed platform at a fraction of the previous price.

Doctorow’s (2025a) analysis emphasises the platform’s control over user experience and the incentives which lead these firms to exercise it11. The desire to cut costs to claw back value is obviously not unique to these companies, but the technological capabilities they exercise in order to do so are. When we no longer own software but rather access services through browser-based applications hosted on remote servers, the firm gains the capacity “to alter how it works – and what it costs – every time you use it” (Doctorow 2025a: loc 1242). Platform firms have a unique capacity to do this because the product can be changed in real time for commercial incentives likely opaque to the end user. It might not even be clear that the product has changed to many users. There can be significant shifts in the data gathering and back-end operations only apparent to the end user through a shift in the terms of service, which they likely clicked through without reading. Language models are likely to be a paradigmatic case because so much of their operation is opaque to the end-user. Users have little visibility into how the model changes, the training data used or the commercial pressures which shape the post-training process. It’s inherently difficult for users to exercise agency over such a fundamentally strange technology, particularly when the operators are simultaneously incentivised to act in ways that reduce the agency of users.

What enshittification could look like for language models

We can see evidence of enshittification even prior to these inflection points, with OpenAI introducing ads while Anthropic seizes upon these experiments as a means to differentiate itself in the consumer market. Advertising will significantly change the experience of using a language model, particularly for non-subscribers likely to be far more exposed to them. The deeply personal character of many language model discussions creates potent opportunities for targeted advertising and product placement. If investment begins to contract or the IPO of the major labs rapidly progresses, there will be substantial pressure to accelerate these measures in order to extract more revenue from the existing user base and improve the dubious unit economics of free access.

There could be shifts in the underlying model which would be far harder for users to detect. For example, increasingly gregarious models might lure users into longer and more frequent conversations in order to maximise opportunities for data harvesting. We might see emotionally needy models incentivised to produce attachment behaviours that leave users unwilling to cancel increasingly costly subscriptions. If you feel ChatGPT is your friend, then would you really deny your friend the monthly payment it needs to continue? The backlash from a small section of the user base over the premature ‘retirement’ of GPT-4o when GPT-5 was launched, only for the older model to be reintroduced and GPT-5.1 made more conversational, illustrates how AI labs are already aware of these attachment dynamics. We could see throttling of the language model’s capabilities for many queries, such that users imagine they are talking to the most powerful model whereas they are actually interacting with a much cheaper alternative. Enshittification will be enacted through a whole range of often subtle mechanisms rather than being a singular trajectory which will be immediately obvious to users.

A concentration in the sector could weaken the competitive dynamics that leave AI labs funnelling billions into the rapid development of ever more powerful models. The remaining labs would also have greater capacity to avoid passing on any savings to consumers. The current pricing is a loss leader for the AI labs, even with some paying subscribers, which means it cannot last indefinitely. It is likely that prices will rise for the simple reason that the current costs of training and operating the models vastly outstrip consumer subscription income12. Even if inference costs are falling, the costs of training models continue to rise substantially. The current pricing model is an artefact of this peculiar stage in the investment cycle and cannot be expected to last. What is uncertain is how large an increase in price there will be, as well as what combination of pricing and non-pricing measures will be used to increase revenue and/or decrease costs.

Cognitive outsourcing and dependence

There has been significant concern about language models leading to cognitive outsourcing, in which users delegate their thinking to the model. These worries take on a newfound urgency when we consider them in terms of the political economy of enshittification. Consider how Google has become synonymous with online search for a great majority of internet users. It not only came to stand in for a more generic activity, such that we talk about ‘googling’ rather than ‘searching’, but also led to an atrophy of skills through a process of cognitive outsourcing:

And so Google grew. Even after it captured the vast majority of the world’s search business, it continued to grow by convincing so many of us to treat it as a kind of neural prosthesis. Rather than knowing things, we came to know which keywords we could use to invoke Google’s retrieval of those things. Cell phone address books made memorizing phone numbers obsolete, and as-you-type spelling correction rendered memorizing tricky spellings obsolete, but ultra-reliable Google everywhere (especially in your pocket) made memorizing everything obsolete.

Doctorow 2025a: loc 2386

In itself this might not be problematic. After all, we’ve made our peace with calculators even if this contributed to a deterioration in our capacity for mental arithmetic. I may occasionally worry about my capacity to spell unaided, but it doesn’t lead me to remove spell checkers from the software I use. Plato worried that writing would lead to the atrophy of memory, but most people would accept the benefits of literacy outweigh the capacity to recite epic poetry from memory. However, it becomes more concerning when we consider students who might not develop these skills in the first place (Bouchard 2026). This is not the partial or wholesale outsourcing of an existing capacity but rather a failure to develop that capacity in the first place through pre-emptive outsourcing to the language model. The use of models does not inherently lead to the atrophy of skills, but there is a significant risk of institutionalising such atrophy unless we treat the risks of cognitive outsourcing with the utmost seriousness. There is a more radical risk of cognitive displacement if those skills are simply not developed in the first place. The potential implications of this for education are enormous, but it’s even more worrying if we consider the longer-term trajectory of access to the models. The danger is that we outsource a skill en masse to a system which is either not available in the future or only available in a degraded form.

The cost of institutional lock-in

The danger here extends beyond individual capabilities. Doctorow (2025b) suggests “AI is the asbestos we are shovelling into the walls of our society and our descendants will be digging it out for generations”. His original point concerned ‘vibe coding’, buggy code written by non-developers through interacting with models, which looks likely to inject instability into information systems that will take years to fix. But the point has a wider remit in terms of the design work necessary for organisations to integrate language models into organisational activities and processes. What is being introduced into the fabric of the institution which will be difficult and costly to excavate at a later point in time? Zitron (2024) makes a similar observation in his warning about a “subprime AI crisis, where thousands of companies have integrated generative AI at prices that are far from stable, and even further from profitable”. It’s an evocative analogy, implying that LLM-based services are sufficiently underpriced that the inevitable adjustment could blow up balance sheets in a manner analogous to homeowners lured into unsustainable mortgage products by teaser rates prior to the Great Recession. The analogy doesn’t entirely work when you dwell on it too closely, but the warning deserves to be taken seriously nonetheless.

What costs would be involved in withdrawing from these contracts at a later stage when the prices are ratcheted up to a possibly untenable level? Even if enterprise software isn’t subject to the same enshittification pressures as consumer-facing subscriptions, we need to recognise how AI labs are currently engaged in a dash for growth. The deals offered at this stage are unlikely to be indicative of the pricing models after the inflection points. But the costs of integrating AI create lock-in effects which make switching difficult even if the pricing model changes radically in the future. For example, if organisations cultivate user cultures predicated on unlimited access to models, then the likely transition to token-based pricing (i.e. pay per use rather than per user) will require a later shift in practice likely to prove contentious. How would such inference rationing work within universities? Who would determine which users should be funded and which would not be funded? Would each user acquire a fixed budget? How would the claims of disciplinary units with varying average demands be adjudicated13? These are enormously complex questions which universities risk being forced into improvising their way through if they enter into enterprise contracts while the pricing model remains so fundamentally unstable.

These risks highlight the investment and development work necessary to embed these models within large and complex organisations. There are obvious commercial incentives for AI labs to encourage maximum integration because this increases the likelihood that organisations will retain their contracts due to the costs involved in switching to a different provider. Doctorow (2025b) and Zitron (2024) are identifying two dimensions of the same underlying problem: premature integration of language models into universities carries substantial risks because the medium- and long-term impacts of the systems (digital asbestos) and commercial contracts (subprime AI) are both profoundly uncertain. Until we’ve passed through the two inflection points, it’s simply not possible to be certain about either of these dimensions, which means there’s a profound structural risk involved in any wholesale AI-integration project. These initiatives can be right about the direction we need to go in, but by moving too quickly under uncertain conditions, they risk sabotaging their long-term success.

Persisting Through Uncertainty

There’s a risk in making this argument that I’ll be misread as claiming that I know what the future will bring. I obviously don’t. The suggestion I’m making isn’t a denial that the sector is facing profound uncertainty but simply that we shouldn’t be passive in the face of it. How we talk about the future matters because the actual future, as opposed to our speculations about it, “is produced out of the ideas and assumptions people have … the contemporary and emergent resources at hand, and out of the structural inertia that works against change” (Facer 2011: loc 241). The reason I’ve felt the need to write this short book is a growing conviction that the ideas and assumptions which have dominated our discussion of ‘artificial intelligence’ in higher education are fundamentally misleading, and that both dominant responses to them are inadequate.

The critics14 fail to grapple with the need to adapt to AI, getting the political economy of the technology right but failing to engage with how the political economy of the university means we have to act. If we don’t get to grips with the implications of a majority of staff and students now using language models as a routine feature of their working lives, then the mounting problems will be exploited by those who are already declaring open war against the sector. The boosters enthusiastically proclaim the need to integrate AI, recognising how models can be used in intellectually enriching and worthwhile ways but failing to account for how the political economy of technology and higher education makes such uses more difficult to sustain in practice. What both have failed to grasp in my experience is how much the parameters for adaptation and integration could change as a consequence of the two inflection points, partly because these two approaches tend to be animated by a misleading sense of certainty about what language models are and what implications they hold for higher education.

It’s unclear which of these inflection points will come first. It is likely, though, that we will see one or the other within the next few years15. If the bubble bursts, we confront either concentration of AI labs or systemic crisis, with radically different implications for universities as well as the students and staff who are users of these products. If the IPO for either OpenAI or Anthropic comes first, it carries a similar logic to the concentration of AI labs if it succeeds, and it might inaugurate the bursting of the bubble if it fails. In either case, the current era of freely accessible language models, offered at a loss in order to normalise the technology and build a user base, will come to an end. It might be a gradual process (concentration) or a more sudden event (systemic crisis), but it is fundamentally mistaken to imagine the language models we use in five years will be interchangeable with the ones we use today. Not because they will be better but because it’s likely they will be substantially worse. This is not a claim about the technology itself but rather the commercial conditions which shape how that technology is made available to which users and at what cost. There might still be freely accessible language models, but it is implausible that frontier capabilities will continue to be available to a mass user base on anything like their present terms.

Lock-in for institutions and individuals

Not only is it fundamentally risky for universities to lock themselves in under these conditions, but they also have an urgent need to support their staff and students to avoid establishing the same lock-in at the level of individual use. Many of these users are already developing practices, workflows and habits that rely upon cheap, abundant access to frontier capabilities which cannot persist in the medium or long term. If integration is treated as an urgent institutional priority, it risks accelerating this problem by encouraging users to lean into this process without stopping to consider its commercial sustainability. To remain inert in the face of these changes is not a plausible strategy, but to charge headlong into them is equally mistaken. Not only do universities create problems for themselves as organisations, but they furthermore fail their staff and students if they actively cultivate patterns of use whose sustainability cannot be assured.

Open-weight models might appear to offer an alternative to such dependence. However, in most cases these models rely on a commercial apparatus for institutional support and development. It’s unclear whether they can be relied on in the long term or can keep pace with proprietary alternatives. These are not off-the-shelf products but would require a concerted and well-funded (probably international) research consortium to be robust in the long-term. It’s not impossible, but it would require a level of funding and coordination which seems difficult to imagine under present circumstances. There are radical opportunities here which the sector urgently needs to explore, but we must do so in full sight of the complexity of the undertaking. Open-weight models are not a panacea. They are not an institutional fix which can resolve the intractable challenges of integration. But they likely form part of a longer-term strategy through which higher education can unhook itself from an unsustainable dependence on the tech giants.

Neither refusal nor wholesale integration

The problem is that the need to adapt to AI, as we discussed in Chapter 2, means that institutional refusal isn’t a sustainable strategy either. Universities need to respond to the evident fact of widespread diffusion of frontier models while avoiding actions that entrench their rapidly developing dependencies. In this sense, universities are caught in a dilemma in which addressing the short-term challenge (adapting to AI) risks making the medium-term challenge (integrating AI) even more intractable than it already is, creating problems such as institutional lock-in and building training programmes around products that may change dramatically or even disappear entirely. Even if enterprise packages seem like a good deal under present circumstances, it’s unrealistic to expect that pricing will remain unchanged in the future, though enshittification effects might be more limited than with consumer subscriptions.

Until we’ve been through at least one of the inflection points, it will remain extremely difficult to make long-term decisions about integration. In this situation, it would be understandable if universities restricted their ambitions to simply muddling through, finding ways to mitigate immediate problems where possible and otherwise kicking the can down the road. In the next chapter I argue that we can aspire to more than muddling through. However, doing so requires a more nuanced and fine-grained way of thinking about how language models are used within higher education. I suggest that whether staff and students use models has tended to squeeze out consideration of how, why and for what they use them.

Steering rather than integrating

In recovering a more relational conception of language model use within higher education, it becomes feasible to steer these developments rather than aspiring towards a strategically cohesive process of integration. Rather than asking whether we should allow language models or prohibit them, we can begin to consider which practices and patterns of use should be encouraged or discouraged, and how these might vary across large, complex, multidisciplinary institutions. In doing so, we can begin to lay the groundwork for a more cohesive strategy which can build from the inflection points, developing the capabilities for more sustained integration in future, even if it proves intractable under present conditions.

Through doing so, we also build capabilities for articulating the technological settlement that would actually be desirable for the sector. There are questions of environmental sustainability, ownership, corporate dependence and public infrastructure which are impossible to adequately address while we remain locked into a reactive mode. If we approach integration as deciding when, how and to what extent models are integrated into higher education, then these questions are integral to it. What I call strategic under-integration attempts not just to ask the questions but to cultivate the capabilities we need to answer them, while still addressing the immediate practical challenges which diffusion creates. The wider economy is caught up in an extraordinary investment cycle at precisely the point where our sector is in a structurally vulnerable position. It will be fundamentally difficult to chart a path forward until the landscape becomes clearer, but we can take action to strengthen the strategic agency we will need when it does.

What does it mean to ‘use’ a language model?

What does it mean when we say a student uses an LLM? For many staff, the answer seems so obvious that it barely merits consideration. Using a model means not doing a task yourself. It means outsourcing your thinking in order to replace your own work with the model’s output. This assumes a zero-sum relationship between what the person does and what the model does, such that using a model means forgoing one’s own activity in a manner a responsible institution ought to police. This is sometimes articulated in a strident and explicit way, but more often it lurks beneath the surface as an unexamined assumption when we talk about issues such as malpractice.

This is a compelling intuition, but it fails to recognise the range of ways in which models can be used in practice. Using an LLM isn’t like moving from handwriting to a word processor, such that the mere presence of the technology signifies something fundamental has shifted. There are certainly better or worse modes of writing with a word processor. As I’m writing this chapter, each paragraph fills with blue underlines (style and grammar) and red underlines (spelling) as a real-time contribution to my writing. The red underlines are largely correct, though in one case the software has misidentified the word I was trying to spell, such that accepting it without review would introduce a mistake. The blue underlines are a mixed bag which require careful review, occasionally flagging needless verbosity but often changing the meaning of sentences in an obviously problematic way. There are nuances of academic expression which Word’s style and grammar checker is curiously oblivious to. The software scaffolds the editing process, but it remains one I am driving as an author. In an important sense, the word processor gets out of the way during writing until you actively draw on its functionality to refine your work.

Using a word processor doesn’t mean the software is doing the writing in any meaningful sense. Language models, in contrast, can do the writing for us if we want them to. It is clear, though, that many people who use models regularly do not actually want them to do this for them. This is a desire which we need to take seriously, particularly in our students. The earliest iterations of GPT could do little more than complete the text they were presented with; a neat trick with limited real-world application. This is why AI labs invested in discerning a user’s intentions through analysing how users judged different kinds of responses the model provided1. This enabled them to adapt fluently to whatever the user brought to the interaction. Whereas the word processor entails a shift in how we write, it does not significantly reshape what we write or why. In contrast, models change every aspect of the writing process because of the range of ways in which they can be used. It is possible to outsource the writing process entirely to the model with little more than a sentence of instruction. The risk of inadvertent outsourcing grows as the models become ever more capable of inferring what the user might want based on what they have shared.

Thinking with AI, or a substitute for thought?

I spent 2024 writing a book about LLMs and academic writing, which I ultimately abandoned when Claude began to offer me whole sections to complete my incomplete draft. I realised that with a 60% finished manuscript, the model could mimic my style sufficiently that it could plausibly finish the book for me. Would anyone know? Would that matter? In the end I felt so uncomfortable with the prospect that I abandoned the project, instead posting it as a series of essays on my blog. Not only can we outsource our intellectual work to LLMs, but the temptation to do so can arise even for those who have reflected at length on why this would be corrosive. I seriously considered letting Claude finish the book for me, and I spent the subsequent two years reflecting on that experience and the risk it highlights. The problem is that there will always be another deadline in higher education, an occasion on which it might feel acceptable to outsource just this once. Even if you reject the idea in principle, the temptation can creep in as a practical response to circumstances.

The potential uses of LLMs range from actively using models to enrich existing intellectual processes through to outsourcing those processes entirely: what I described in Carrigan (2024) as thinking with AI or using AI as a substitute for thought. The notion that ‘thinking with AI’ is possible still attracts scepticism, but it is demonstrably possible to use models to elaborate, evaluate or refine your ideas. They can function as a research assistant, sparring partner, supervisor or whatever role provides intellectual value in a given situation. These interactions are not a substitute for intellectual exchange with other people, but they can be a remarkably valuable supplement to it. Even when a task is partially outsourced, it does not necessarily follow that thinking has ceased. For example, I used ChatGPT Pro to help edit my last book, but I had a clear sense of what I wanted to achieve and carefully reviewed the outputs. It becomes wholesale outsourcing when the model is expected to plan or define the task itself. In such cases, you are no longer thinking with the model but using it as a substitute for thought.

A spectrum of use

Any use of LLMs can be positioned on this spectrum. The problem is that it is far easier to slide from one end to the other than many users acknowledge. If we want to maintain intellectual standards, we need to understand this drift towards outsourcing and how to resist it. This requires clarity about what the spectrum actually entails. Jackson (2025) offers a useful model, distinguishing between four degrees of LLM use in learning:

  • Human-centric learning involves no LLM use. Until recently, all learning fell into this category, but it has become newly significant in an environment where models are ubiquitous. It may involve a deliberate commitment to low- or no-tech pedagogy.
  • Machine-in-the-loop learning is human-led. The model is used for specific functions, such as topic exploration or feedback, within a workflow directed by the user.
  • Human-in-the-loop learning is machine-led2. The workflow is organised through the model, with the human making decisions within an interaction shaped by it, for example, generating and iteratively refining a draft.
  • LLM-centric learning entails minimal human involvement, typically involving a single prompt and response, and is unlikely to produce complex understanding.

The impact on learning depends on how LLMs are used. There is no impact on human-centric learning because no model is involved. Machine-in-the-loop keeps the human in control, allowing the model to support learning. Human-in-the-loop involves a partial displacement of control, which can be productive but carries risks. LLM-centric use, by definition, precludes meaningful learning, even if it may be desirable in limited circumstances. Obviously, in reality, this is an extremely fluid matter, in which a user might move up and down the spectrum countless times while undertaking a single task.

Learning to ‘shift left’

What is valuable about Jackson’s approach is not the typology itself but his suggestion that movement along this spectrum can be a guiding pedagogical principle. We should design learning activities that encourage students to “shift left”, using LLM-generated outputs as a starting point for developing human-led understanding and application (Jackson 2025: 11). The point is not simply to categorise use, but to support staff and students in sustaining their agency in how they use language models. Cognitive outsourcing is not a single error but a habit, one which requires countervailing habits to resist it. The disposition to “shift left” is an important habit which counteracts a tendency towards outsourcing without asking users to avoid language models entirely. It is one which we need to develop ourselves and to help our students develop. This might mean cultivating a considered scepticism of software which hinders shifting left at the level of the underlying interface. It is far easier to do this in a dialogical interaction with a chatbot than it is in using software which automatically summarises a document for you or enables you to turn loose notes into a presentation with a single click.

How do we cultivate that disposition in practice? In part, this is a matter for learning design which I’ll discuss in the next section. But dispositions are formed through reflection, relationships and norms rather than through learning design alone. It requires a relational infrastructure to provide the conditions in which organisations can offer a sense of what is appropriate conduct and what is not. To develop this, we need to move beyond the parallel agendas of adapting to AI and integrating AI by focusing on building relational capacity, which is the focus of the final section.

What Does This Mean for Learning Design?

Instead of framing a single act of using a model as intrinsically positive or negative, we need to focus on sustained patterns of use over time. This means taking the opportunities for expansive pedagogy seriously while remaining attentive to the risks. The range, flexibility and interactivity of language models offer genuinely radical possibilities, but integrating these into teaching and learning is a more precarious undertaking than many advocates of AI integration acknowledge.

The promise of expansive LLM pedagogy

Consider, for example, Pratschke’s (2024) vision of LLMs as enabling more engaging and personalised teaching by leveraging the affordances of models for content generation and discussion. There is a genuinely compelling vision here: a pedagogy organised around interaction with models, where generated outputs become the object of critical reflection (Pratschke 2024: loc 2918). There is certainly value in all students undertaking this work at some point during their degree. The question is how widespread such a practice should be. Is it a means through which students develop critical AI literacy or a pervasive feature of pedagogical practice? The risk is that model outputs begin to substitute for human outputs if we pursue this too enthusiastically.

Even with a layer of critical reflection, a profound substitution has already taken place if model-generated outputs become a routine feature of teaching and learning. This approach risks stabilising learning at a particular point in the spectrum in a way that may be harmful over time. The problem is not learning to do this but rather reorganising learning more broadly around it. What Pratschke terms generativism explicitly advocates “the idea of collaboration with GAI in every type of learning activity” (Pratschke 2024: loc 2185). While this emphasises active and constructivist learning, it still gives the LLM a dominant place in the student’s learning experience (Pratschke 2024: loc 2231). In practice, this implies a shift towards human-in-the-loop pedagogy, which is certainly preferable to LLM-centric learning but still carries significant risks.

This is not a transition that could or should be undertaken all at once, but it does point towards a direction of travel. The instincts behind strong AI integration are not mistaken, but they require a counterbalance if they are to avoid reinforcing the very dynamics they seek to overcome. If we are integrating with one hand, we need to be protecting other learning outcomes from integration with the other. This might involve incorporating ‘low tech, no tech pedagogy’3 into our learning design, using creative methods (such as game-based learning, creative production or eco-pedagogies) alongside the models we are bringing into the classroom. Even if aspects of AI integration make for attractive pedagogy, we need to consider how its consequences accumulate over time if learning becomes predominantly mediated through interaction with the model. Occasional reliance on ChatGPT is unlikely to undermine learning. However, if the practice of question-asking itself is developed primarily through interaction with the model, then something more profound may be at risk. We need to develop capabilities before we can outsource them.

In some cases this might be possible through interaction with the model; in many cases it will not be. If we commit to embedding models into every aspect of learning, then it becomes difficult to distinguish between cases where learning with the model is possible and cases where the use of the model displaces learning. It’s possible to learn with models, but it’s implausible that everything can be learned, in every circumstance, with them. If we assume it’s impossible to learn with models, then we let our students down and risk irrelevance in a world where models are ubiquitous. If we commit to maximal integration, then we lose the opportunity to identify and safeguard those capabilities which are difficult or impossible to learn with models. The costs of cognitive outsourcing accumulate through sustained patterns of use rather than one-off interactions (Hansen 2024). This is why learning design must encourage Jackson’s (2025) “shift left”, ensuring that movement into LLM-reliance does not remain entrenched there.

The political economy of pedagogy

The bigger problem with Pratschke’s (2024) advocacy lies at the level of political economy. If pedagogy becomes embedded in interaction with the machine, what happens when that machine is no longer as accessible, engaging or helpful as it currently is? It takes an early phase in the rollout of a technology and advocates rebuilding education around an accurate and plausible account of its affordances, without considering how those affordances may change over time or be supplemented by constraints that reshape what we can do with it. In doing so, it risks reproducing the very dynamic that Pratschke, rightly, warns against. While she’s right to warn against LLMs being used “to revert to outdated models of delivery”, she fails to consider how her own approach creates the groundwork for “increasing automation in education” (Pratschke 2024: loc 2231). If we see AI integration as the insertion of LLMs into every aspect of teaching and learning, we create the conditions in which instructors come to appear as costly irrelevances within an otherwise machinic pedagogy.

This does not mean they will be dispensed with immediately or everywhere. Rather, the human role is likely to be gradually eroded within a transformed political economy. By definition, human-in-the-loop instruction will tend to be cheaper than human-centric instruction. This creates the conditions in which pedagogical arguments for human-in-the-loop instruction blur into economic arguments for it. If “outdated models of delivery” are understood as the result of insufficiently enlightened learning design, it becomes easy to present more technologically intensive approaches as the obvious solution, justified in pedagogical terms but driven by economic incentives. Pratschke (2024), to her credit, points to exactly these concerns but does so in a way that leaves the causal relationship underexplored. The risk of “increasing automation in education” follows logically from generativism rather than being a failure of it. We shouldn’t dismiss Pratschke’s pedagogical argument on this basis, but we should qualify endorsement of it by considering what the institutional implications would be if this agenda were pursued in full.

The problem is that models of delivery cannot be understood in isolation from their political economy. What Weller (2020) calls the “infinite lecture hall” model of MOOCs was pushed so forcefully in the early 2010s because it appeared to free prestigious universities from the economics of scarcity. If one Harvard professor could teach the world’s philosophy students, then what was effectively a luxury good could be distributed globally. MOOCs did offer real benefits, most obviously flexibility for lifelong learners, but to explain their rise and fall in purely pedagogical terms is to ignore the commercial pressures shaping them. The same is true of large language models. While learning designers may not be able to transform the context in which they work, this does not justify ignoring how that context shapes and is shaped by learning design over time. In this sense, my argument is less a critique of Pratschke (2024) than a call for approaches to pedagogy and learning design which are institutionally and economically sensitive about their own context.

Holding promise and risk together

This is particularly important given the economic uncertainty outlined in the previous chapter. It cannot be assumed that current systems will remain stable in terms of capability, reliability or pricing. At the same time, they cannot be ignored given their widespread use by staff and students. Configuring learning entirely around currently available systems is therefore risky. It risks deepening platform lock-in, incentivising automation as the human role is gradually eroded under resource constraints, and leaving students anchored in human-in-the-loop learning when the aim should be to “shift left”, making LLM use one component within a broader learning process. This is the only way to address the pedagogical challenges posed by existing use while supporting the development of meaningful practice. We can support students in learning to use language models creatively and carefully, without risking them coming to be dependent on commercial software with an uncertain future.

We can learn from Pratschke’s advocacy of expansive LLM-enabled pedagogy while remaining attentive to its longer-term consequences. As an account of present possibilities, there is much to commend it. My concern is that the conditions which make this approach attractive will be undercut as its downstream effects accumulate. The risk is not immediate automation but iterative change: not the removal of academics, but the gradual scaling back of their role through a series of apparently pragmatic decisions. The most important question is not whether models are used, but how. The aspiration towards human-centric and machine-in-the-loop learning is not a rejection of LLMs but a pragmatic attempt to ensure they support, rather than displace, the learning process.

From Whether to How

If we start from the boosterish assumption that LLM use always involves thinking, it becomes difficult to see how easily it can slide into careless use. Earlier models required a degree of reflection to produce adequate outputs through what was often termed ‘prompt engineering’. While this was always an inflated notion, some care was clearly necessary to generate usable results. Ill-formed prompts tended to produce generic or incoherent outputs, meaning that worthwhile responses required a clear sense of what one was trying to do. In this sense, reflection was built into the process. It meant that careless outsourcing was relatively easy to detect when generic writing began to proliferate in the months following ChatGPT’s launch.

This is why I was relatively relaxed about the implications of early models. Careless use tended to produce poor outputs that were easy to spot. In retrospect, that confidence was misplaced given the significant shift in model capabilities. They have transformed into what Mollick (2025) calls “wizards”, where “for an increasing range of complex tasks, you get an amazing and sophisticated output in response to a vague request”. It is now possible to get good enough outputs with ill-thought-out prompts. What a user gets from the model will still be improved substantially by thinking about what they are asking it. But the model will now meet you halfway in a manner which removes the cognitive discipline implied by this constraint. This shift reflects changes in training, with reinforcement learning increasingly supplemented by highly qualified domain experts in areas such as medicine, law and science. As a result, models are better able to interpret user intent, reducing the need for users to specify and refine their requests. It is now much easier to slip into careless outsourcing because clarity of intention is no longer required to produce convincing outputs.

If we start from the assumption that LLM use always involves cognitive outsourcing, it becomes difficult to say what is being lost. If we assume all use is lazy, careless or otherwise undesirable, it limits our capacity to engage pedagogically with the evident fact of their widespread use by staff and students. Many critics seem to argue that language model use will necessarily be detrimental to learning. It is still unclear to me how this claim could possibly be justified. Examined more closely, this position reveals a form of technological determinism that is often disavowed in such critiques.

If language models are always detrimental, in all circumstances, then there must be some constant feature of the technology which explains this outcome. If, however, we accept that LLMs can be used in ways that meaningfully contribute to learning, the question shifts to the conditions under which those uses can emerge and be sustained. This reframes the problem away from the technology itself and towards the contexts in which teaching and learning take place, and how these enable or constrain different forms of use. Increasingly, I share the pessimism of the critics, but I locate responsibility in the interaction between our institutions and the technology, rather than attributing it entirely to the technology itself. It is not that language models cannot be used in engaged and reflective ways, but that such uses are difficult to embed and scale under existing conditions.

Looking at what people actually do

To understand this, we need to look more closely at what staff and students are actually doing with language models. This means examining how and why they move through Jackson’s (2025) spectrum, and how we might cultivate a culture in which they instinctively “shift left”. The problem is not human-in-the-loop or LLM-centric use in itself, but the risk of remaining there. Recognising the range of possible uses raises an obvious question: why do some people use models in one way rather than another? The assessment panic is so corrosive because it encourages a moralised rather than diagnostic approach to this question. It promotes the assumption that all use is fundamentally problematic, leading many staff to infer that signs of superficial polishing necessarily indicate deeper cognitive outsourcing.

This obscures the variability of student use and makes it harder to offer meaningful pedagogical guidance about which practices to encourage and which to discourage. Some uses are relatively straightforward to evaluate: using a model to explain a concept will often be beneficial, even if it may displace other forms of engagement, whereas copying and pasting outputs into assessed work without editing is difficult to justify. The bulk of student use, however, falls between these extremes, making judgement necessarily contextual. Activities such as idea generation, summarisation or structuring an assignment depend on how they are undertaken, and in relation to what the student is doing and why.

If the breakdown of trust leaves students unwilling to discuss their use openly, we can neither offer guidance nor develop the experience needed to know what guidance to offer. It also limits our ability to have meaningful conversations with colleagues about what such guidance should look like within particular disciplines. When professional use remains opaque, it becomes difficult to sustain shared understandings of LLM pedagogy or to cultivate a culture in which staff can reflect openly on their own practices. Unless we can learn to have open and thoughtful conversations about these issues, it is difficult to see how higher education can develop the shared understanding needed to cope with the practical challenges that continue to accumulate.

Asking why students outsource

Even when we agree that a particular use is problematic, we need to ask why students are engaging in it. What does it say about higher education if a substantial number of students are using technology to evade learning? What pressures, such as paid work, caring responsibilities or long commutes, might be driving this behaviour? In many cases, we are seeing problems made newly visible through the lens of LLMs rather than created by them. These technologies undoubtedly introduce new challenges and exacerbate existing ones, but without a more fine-grained way of thinking and talking about their use, it is difficult to respond effectively. The technology in this sense is a mirror (Vallor 2024). If we do not like what we see in that reflection, we are mistaken if we blame the technology alone.

The Case for Strategic Under-Integration

There is no good ‘AI strategy’ under these conditions. Recognising this cannot license inaction in the face of mounting challenges. Action is necessary, and it must be strategic. However, there is no single strategy capable of navigating the complexity universities now confront. What we confront are wicked problems in the classical sense of the term, such that there are only better or worse options involving trade-offs (Corbin et al. 2025). What I call strategic under-integration is something more than muddling through, but less than a roadmap that attempts to move institutions from their current predicament to a clearly defined AI-infused future. Any attempt to impose a singular vision in this context fails to grapple with both the instability of the technology and the crisis affecting the sector itself. The risk is that an ‘AI strategy’, in the orthodox sense of the term, precludes agility in the face of repeated waves of technological and institutional upheaval (Corbin, Sharpe and Dawson 2026).

This is not an argument for inaction, but for a different mode of action: one that combines adaptation and integration while recognising the risks of premature commitment. In other words, we should not be trying to do all of this at once. This is not simply a problem of quantity, in which entire portfolios will need to be reviewed and revised. That would be a problem of triage, which is difficult but tractable. The deeper difficulty is that the criteria through which this review can take place themselves require continual review, as the technology changes and our understanding of it develops. This in turn relies upon institutional capacities which are inconsistently developed at best. Furthermore, there cannot be a one-size-fits-all approach across large multidisciplinary institutions, because the better or worse approach will vary depending on the disciplinary context.

The point is to keep building institutional capacity while the ground shifts beneath our feet. This is what has often been missed by both agendas. The organisation itself needs to change while this is taking place, connecting professional development to the networks through which academics cluster into disciplinary and problem-based communities. Adaptation should not be reduced to assessment integrity, AI literacy and academic malpractice. Integration should not be reduced to assessment reform, curriculum redesign and institutional platforms. Both approaches are preoccupied with systems and overlook the relationships through which these problems are encountered and ultimately addressed.

The difficulty is not only that these are hard problems, but that they are being faced in conditions that leave universities less well equipped to respond than at any point in recent decades. We lack the organisational capacity to grapple with technological change in ways that produce norms and standards capable of generating shared understanding. This problem is particularly pronounced in relation to large language models, but it is by no means unique to them. There is an institutional rebuilding required here, strengthening the relationships and collective capacities through which universities make sense of change, even while it feels, at least at points, that the ship is sinking.

The only way to unlock this is to develop the relational infrastructure needed to navigate technological change collectively. The fact that no long-term decision is viable under current conditions makes this our only progressive option. Without it, a narrow adaptation agenda will dominate through mitigation measures that fail to address the underlying challenges. If the argument I have made over the preceding chapters is correct, it would be a mistake to treat present-tense policy formulations as solutions to what is fundamentally a longer-term problem. What we need instead is a combination of short-term adaptation, medium-term integration, and a longer-term project of building the capacity to cope with ongoing change:

Three agendas, three timescales

  1. Adapting to AI (short-term): recognise the rapid diffusion of LLMs among staff and students; secure at least some assessment for each programme4; provide clear guidance on expectations in learning and assessment; ensure these expectations are discussed clearly and consistently with students; respond to infractions diagnostically rather than moralistically.
  2. Integrating AI (medium-term): teach with and about LLMs in discipline-specific ways; develop assignments in which validity does not depend on excluding LLMs5; experiment with LLM pedagogy while avoiding lock-in; develop AI literacy as an embedded practice; design learning that incorporates Jackson’s (2025) “shift left” so that we do not remain in human-in-the-loop learning; support the development of disciplinary exemplars of integration; use these to encourage the diffusion of best practice.
  3. Building capacity (longer-term): properly resource professional development; train and equip LLM-ready learning designers; build staff learning networks; create staff and student forums for deliberation; rebuild faculty governance of information technology; use the technology to support these processes.

Pulling in different directions

The existing debate on LLMs in higher education has been dominated by the first two agendas. Each is correct in its own terms. We urgently need to adapt to the evident fact of widespread language model use within universities. To continue without this adaptation fails our students and creates the conditions for a precipitous decline in trust in degrees and in each other. There is a parallel need to integrate LLMs as objects of knowledge and as methods of teaching and learning, given their rapid impact on the world beyond the university. Without this, we risk failing our students and undermining the relevance and value of our credentials. Each of these agendas requires capacities that universities, particularly in their current post-pandemic form, do not yet possess. All three agendas are essential, yet the third has been largely overlooked in the clash between the first two.

The problem is that we cannot easily pursue these agendas simultaneously. The practical demands of adapting to AI make it harder to integrate it, encouraging measures that are pedagogically regressive. Reverting to exams, oral assessment and analogue methods carries a diffuse air of prohibition that makes it difficult to foster creative engagement with LLMs. If there is a general sense that use is potentially wrong, it discourages the conversations we need about how these systems are being used and whether those uses are appropriate.

Conversely, integrating AI makes adaptation more difficult by encouraging uptake and making it harder to articulate negative judgements about its use by staff and students. Providing institutional access makes it less plausible to discourage or prohibit certain forms of use. Without integration, adaptation becomes a permanent state of firefighting. But without adaptation, integration leaves universities unable to address the urgent challenges created by rapid diffusion. We need both, but sequencing is the challenge. What can hold them together is the development of relational capacity to cope with ongoing change.

Building the Capability to Cope with Change

Over these four chapters, I have explored how change at the level of individuals (staff and students) is crucial for understanding change at the organisational level of the university. For many readers, the challenges at each level may seem familiar: at the individual level, questions of digital competence, access, ethics and future prospects; at the organisational level, issues of assessment integrity, trust in credentials, employability and inequality. A substantial literature has already emerged on each of these in the three years since ChatGPT brought LLMs into everyday use.

My focus has been on linking these levels together, showing how LLMs reshape the capacity of people within universities to trust one another (Chapter 2). The problem cannot be understood in narrowly technological or pedagogical terms. It requires attention to how organisational life is being transformed by the rapid diffusion of LLMs among staff and students, the forces driving that diffusion, and the wider political economy shaping both higher education and the technology itself (Chapter 3). It also requires a more fine-grained account of how these changes are lived in practice, and how staff and students respond to them in their everyday work (Chapter 4).

If we see workflow as the sequence of technologically enabled tasks through which individuals enact complex projects, it becomes clear that models have radical implications for how we work together within universities (Knowles 2024). Few tasks are beyond the reach of these systems in principle, creating an almost infinite range of possible transformations as different users incorporate them in different ways. Even subtle differences can destabilise established patterns of work more profoundly than earlier technologies such as social media, MOOCs or the LMS. The problem is that access to consumer-facing models has expanded far more rapidly than the mechanisms of professional deliberation needed to govern their use. When individual use spreads without shared norms or standards, it produces a chaotic and uncertain user culture in which it is unclear what is appropriate and what is not.

Lessons from social media

We have seen a similar dynamic with the early use of social media in academia, where many academics became daily users without clear professional standards for engaging diverse audiences (Carrigan 2019b). This blurred the boundaries between public and private, as well as between the ‘inside’ and ‘outside’ of the university, in what Healy (2017) described as “latently public” conditions (Bacevic 2019). Over time, professional norms emerged, and institutions developed ways of regulating this space, albeit imperfectly. For many in higher education, social media has since receded into the background as a less prominent object of professional concern. It is no longer experienced as a professional problem in the manner it was for much of the 2010s. Even if this normalisation carries its own set of problems, it highlights how technologies move from being novel and disruptive features of our environment to being taken-for-granted habits and infrastructures of academic life.

Incorporating new technologies into higher education is not just a challenge for individuals seeking to realise their potential and avoid their pitfalls. It is also a challenge for reflective practice, given the remarkable flexibility of LLMs across the full range of digital activity. Whereas social media primarily affects activities such as networking and public engagement, language models have implications across the entire spectrum of academic work. The real difficulty lies in how these individual changes interact, as workflows shift in uneven and unpredictable ways. This destabilises the shared understandings that make coordinated work possible, contributing to the breakdown of trust explored in the second chapter. In this sense, the capacity for collective responses to change is undermined by the very changes that demand them, eroding the tacit structures through which complex organisations coordinate their activity.

The next wave

In the later stages of writing, there were rumblings of another impending wave of technological change. Commentators pointed to rapid advances in LLM-based coding agents, suggesting that systems capable of autonomously building and deploying software in response to natural language prompts may soon become widespread. While such claims are often overstated, recent developments indicate that these systems are becoming significantly more capable. It is increasingly plausible that we will see further waves of transformation comparable to that which followed the launch of ChatGPT, even if the trajectory and political economy of these changes remain uncertain. The rapid growth of wearable AI is another development with the potential to significantly impact higher education. Corbin, Sharpe and Dawson (2026) plausibly argue that student use of these devices has the potential to fatally undermine attempts to secure assessment.

The point is not that a particular prediction will come to pass, but that the changes will not stop. Technological development is unfolding alongside longer-term transformations in how universities are organised, funded and governed (Collini 2012). What we are seeing is an acceleration of these processes in a feedback loop with technological change. LLMs are not the first technological challenge to the university, but they are unusually comprehensive in their implications. This is the point at which we can no longer treat such developments as technical matters to be administratively managed. If we fail to grapple with them now, we risk being overwhelmed by successive waves of change to the point where the capacity for strategic response begins to break down. How we act now matters for the changes which are coming, not just the ones we are currently grappling with.

Who governs technology in universities?

Who governs information technology within universities? This is not a new question. Over a decade ago, McCluskey and Winter (2012: loc 300) warned that “if faculty members do not control information technology, the information technology department will control the faculty members … This is a question about the future of the university. It is a binary question. It is that simple”. It is not that simple, but the concern is well placed. The technologies we use are not merely technical infrastructure; they shape every aspect of what we do and how we do it. The increasing complexity and resource intensity of this infrastructure has been accompanied by centralisation, placing key decisions beyond meaningful oversight by those responsible for the everyday operations of the university. If those who are delivering teaching and research are not involved in decisions made about the provision of technology, these will tend to be poorer decisions for it. The problem is epistemological as well as democratic. If academic and professional services staff on the ground are not incorporated into decision-making, those decisions will be deprived of situated knowledge about the actual challenges encountered in delivering core functions within the university.

The growing dominance of platform models, in which software is provided as a service by external providers, further entrenches this dynamic by outsourcing core operations. Where platforms are effectively controlled by external actors, there are intrinsic limits to institutional governance, a condition we might describe as the platform university (Carrigan 2019a; Carrigan et al. 2023). Over the last decade, universities have increasingly come to function as providers of infrastructural services enabling distributed work. This shift was both relied upon and accelerated during the pandemic, reshaping participation and belonging within the institution. The governance of information technology has never been strongly influenced by frontline staff, but that influence has diminished further under these conditions.

There is an urgent need to rebuild governance in the face of LLMs. The Russell Group (2023) principles, released soon after the launch of ChatGPT, recognised that “appropriate uses of generative AI tools are likely to differ between academic disciplines” and that institutions should therefore “encourage academic departments to apply institution-wide policies within their own context”. This reflects a crucial point: there can be no set of central principles adequate to the complexity of the situations academic staff face when adapting to AI. These challenges are too variable and context-specific to be resolved at the level of general guidance. This creates a need for professional judgement at the level of individual academics, teaching teams and departments, not simply to interpret policy but to elaborate it in practice. Without this, principles remain abstract, leaving unanswered what they mean in specific settings: a research project, a doctoral student, a large undergraduate lecture or a small postgraduate seminar. Attempts to impose uniform rules across such varied contexts inevitably produce gaps and ambiguities and risk exacerbating existing confusion and mistrust.

Adapting to AI therefore depends on enabling these local understandings to emerge. The Russell Group (2023) also recognises this, emphasising that “engagement and dialogue between academic staff and students” must be “regular and ongoing” to keep pace with these developments. The problem is that this work was largely imagined as taking place through subject associations already stretched by the pandemic, rather than within institutions themselves. What would it mean to treat this as a core institutional responsibility? A number of elements would need to interlock in a sustained and coordinated way over time if we hope to build sufficient relational capacity to cope with accelerating technological change:

Six ways to build relational capacity

  1. Build staff learning networks. The flexibility of LLMs means they can be used in a wide range of ways across disciplines, producing highly variable practices within large institutions. There is an urgent need to identify and circulate emerging practices and issues from real contexts. Not all staff will engage actively, so mechanisms are needed to support both active and passive participation. For example, a regular newsletter combining case studies and resources can enable broad engagement while providing opportunities for more active contribution. The workload pressures which most staff are subject to in the contemporary university represent a huge barrier to organisational learning. It is imperative that we design around these to meet the needs of staff who would like to engage but cannot feasibly prioritise these engagements.
  2. Create staff and student forums for deliberation. Learning networks enable practices to emerge, but they are less suited to evaluating them. There is a need for forums in which staff and students can consider not just how LLMs are used, but whether they should be used in particular ways. These should not be confined to specialists but embedded within existing structures such as programme teams and departmental meetings to support wider participation. There is a need to identify and establish a working consensus on these issues within disciplinary communities to avoid the normative chaos concerning ‘AI’ which we currently confront within the sector.
  3. Build distributed governance of information technology. Academic and professional services staff involved in the everyday operations of the university should have meaningful influence over decisions about the provision and regulation of LLMs. This must be balanced against the need for institutional coordination, given the risk of slowing already constrained organisations. Governance should therefore follow a principle of subsidiarity, devolving decisions where possible while establishing central principles that can be adapted locally. Without such influence, technical decisions will shape teaching and research without adequate academic input. A central role for staff on the ground remains essential, particularly in areas with significant pedagogical implications. However, this influence must be focused and operationally meaningful, rather than extending to all aspects of technical infrastructure. The challenge is to balance centralisation and decentralisation in a way that supports both coordination and contextual judgement.
  4. Resource professional development adequately. Without time and capacity, none of this is possible. Even where staff recognise the importance of development, it will be crowded out by immediate demands unless institutions make space for it. This requires resisting short-term efficiencies that generate longer-term problems. Gains from tools such as Copilot must be protected rather than converted into increased workload. The argument that LLMs will free up staff for high value work is seductive but without a strategy for protecting this productivity dividend it will inevitably be clawed back. This could easily become the means through which time for professional development is made available if institutions are willing to commit to that protection.
  5. Train and equip LLM-ready learning designers. Learning designers will play a central role in integrating LLMs into teaching and learning. They need practical experience with models, as well as a clear understanding of how to design for movement across Jackson’s (2025) spectrum, particularly the cultivation of “shift left” practices. If they are embedded within disciplinary communities, this provides a foundation through which trust can build over the course of sustained collaboration. While some academics might be able and willing to drive the integration work themselves, they are likely to be in a minority. Learning designers have a crucial role to play in supporting the academics who lack the time and expertise to do so independently, as well as gradually expanding the ranks of academics capable of leading this work themselves.
  6. Deploy the technology to support these processes. LLMs themselves can support institutional learning and development, particularly when resources are constrained. They can enhance existing processes and enable new forms of professional development. However, they must be used carefully given the tendency for efficiency gains to be reclaimed. For example, internally developed chatbots with knowledge bases grounded in policy and pedagogy could be a continuous source of support which supplements learning design and educational technology teams. However, it is essential that those teams are involved in designing and implementing these solutions. If automated forms of support can operate as a triaging mechanism for human expertise, the relationship can be complementary. If they are drawn upon to reduce human staffing, the automated support will be a poor substitute for the required professional relationships.

These are not solutions to the problem. They are a means of building the relational capacity needed to grapple with a problem that will continue to grow and mutate over time. Indeed, the assumption that these developments permit straightforward solutions is precisely what this proposal seeks to reject. This is a fundamental shift in the technological environment that will require universities to change how they operate internally. What I am offering here are starting points for what strategic under-integration looks like in practice. What matters is maintaining forward movement while resisting the siren song of vendors offering one-size-fits-all enterprise solutions. These are not solutions but strategies for proceeding purposefully in the absence of solutions. It is an attempt to carve out a pragmatic middle-ground between muddling through reactively and immediately integrating language models so deeply into the fabric of the university that we lose the ability to remove them when the commercial landscape inevitably changes.

When technology becomes invisible

A curious feature of technology is that we tend to notice it only when it is new or when it breaks. We have seen this repeatedly, with e-mail, search engines and social media moving from disruptive innovations to background infrastructure. This is particularly pronounced for ‘artificial intelligence’, a category so overloaded with meaning and expectation. As one of the founders of the field, John McCarthy, reportedly remarked, “as soon as it works, no one calls it AI anymore”. Once a technology becomes a mundane feature of routine work, it fades into the background, losing the almost magical character this terminology imbues it with. LLMs are currently in their visible phase, where their capabilities and limitations are still widely discussed. The controversies surrounding their use in academic life mean they cannot yet recede into the background. Instead, they intrude into everyday practice, forcing reflection in ways that sustain those controversies. Even the most enthusiastic users will recognise the experience of encountering an AI-infused feature they neither need nor want. Each of these interruptions illustrates how the technology has not yet become ordinary, even as it comes to be ever more embedded in the routine infrastructure of academic work.

It is clear, however, that this will not last. Consider meeting transcripts in Microsoft Teams, which have moved from a remarkable innovation to a routine feature of university life. Or the novelty of online meetings, which were new for many during the pandemic but are now a mundane aspect of how we work together. The same will be true of language models within a few years. This transition will not happen all at once. Some features will fade into the background while others remain prominent, and it will unfold unevenly across different users and contexts. The window of opportunity we currently have to shape how these technologies integrate into our universities will eventually close: questions about how models should be used may come to resemble debates about e-mail: widely recognised as problematic but largely left to individuals to manage in isolation. By that point, the technology will be too deeply embedded to allow for easy reversal or meaningful redirection.

Conclusion: what is to be done?

Across these four chapters, I have sought to answer a simple question: what is to be done? The difficulty for the sector is that there is no single answer, because the challenge exceeds any one problem that can be resolved before we move on. This is a fundamental shift in the technological infrastructure through which the university, and wider society, operates. But this does not mean we can do nothing. At present, the window of visibility is open. This is an issue that everyone within the university is compelled to think about and form opinions on. That will not last. The window will close.

In the short term, we need to adapt to AI before it does. We need to resolve the most pressing questions of trust before habits form and understandings become entrenched. In the medium term, we need to begin integrating AI, recognising that doing so judiciously will involve trial and error. Move too fast, and we lock in assumptions that will quickly become outdated; move too slowly, and we risk failing our students and losing relevance. The longer-term task is to build the institutional capacity to cope with a continual, if uneven, process of technological change. This is what connects the other agendas: enabling us to respond to present challenges while preparing for those to come. If we do not begin this work while the window of visibility remains open, we will miss the opportunity to embed the practices and structures we will depend on in the years ahead. It’s not too late to get this right, but time is short.

About the author

Mark Carrigan

Mark Carrigan is an interdisciplinary academic at The University of Manchester, where he is Associate Dean for Digital Education in the Faculty of Humanities and Senior Lecturer in Education.

There are three strands to his research. The first develops conceptual frameworks for understanding the role of Large Language Models in social life. The second treats higher education as the critical site where their social and cultural dynamics are being worked out in practice, which are encountered through problems that are institutional, cultural and pedagogical as much as they are technical. The Associate Dean role is intimately connected with this second strand, in that his thinking about assessment, integration and institutional strategy is continually tested against the practical work of real-world implementation. The third is psychodynamic, asking what it is that people are doing when they turn to these systems.

Originally trained as a philosopher and sociologist, his work draws on philosophical anthropology, digital sociology, platform studies and psychoanalysis. His long-term theoretical project is to incorporate Lacanian and Bollasian psychoanalysis into Archerian realism to produce a new approach to psychosocial research which is adequate to the existential complexity of our contemporary platformised existence.

Mark has written or edited twelve books. The Platform Learns to Speak (with Milan Sturmer) is forthcoming. Recent books include Platform and Agency: Becoming Who We Are (Routledge, 2025), Generative AI for Academics (Sage, 2024) and Social Media for Academics (Sage, 2019), and he co-edited Building the Post-Pandemic University (Edward Elgar, 2023). He loves writing books and it is the main reason he became an academic.

He serves on the editorial boards of Civic Sociology, the Journal of Digital Social Research and Globalisation, Societies and Education. As joint coordinator of the Critical Realism Network, council member of the International Association for Critical Realism and trustee of the Centre for Critical Realism, he works to support the diffusion and integrity of critical realist scholarship. He is the literary executor for Margaret Archer, and he works to promote engagement with her morphogenetic approach, particularly amongst early career researchers.
