A lesson in trust

Early on a Sunday morning in early 2024 I made the mistake of checking my e-mail before I’d had my coffee. What I discovered felt like it would ruin my day: a message from a Boston-based law firm threatening legal action. They claimed I had used a copyrighted image on my blog without permission and demanded immediate restitution to avoid further escalation. It was an unsettling e-mail to receive from what appeared to be a legitimate law firm with a large number of staff and a real presence in the world. However, as I browsed the website and the caffeine hit my system, I rapidly began to realise that something wasn’t quite right about the message. The legal details of the letter appeared to be wrong in a number of ways. There was also something eerily generic about the website that I couldn’t put my finger on. It was only when I searched for the firm and discovered no digital footprint beyond their own website that I realised this was clearly not what it presented itself as being. Once I discovered the domain name had been registered in Iceland the year before, I realised this was obviously a scam. It appeared that chatbots had been used to produce a sufficiently plausible website that it would persuade their targets that the e-mails came from a legitimate law firm.

The e-mail didn’t ruin my day but it did leave me preoccupied by what language models mean for trust. My immediate reaction to the e-mail was to look at the firm’s website. It appeared obviously legitimate to me. Why? It was an extensive site with detailed blurbs and photos of a large and diverse staff. There was even a blog in which those staff opined on a range of legal matters. Even just three years ago it would have been extremely costly to construct such a website in order to deceive an audience. It might have taken a team days to write so much material. Yet now it’s possible for someone to generate the website, text and images in a matter of minutes. What I found chilling about this experience was how lazily it had been put together. It didn’t survive twenty minutes of serious scrutiny but then it wasn’t intended to stand up to investigation. It just needed to persuade a casual viewer that they should take the legal threat seriously.

The proxies we rely on

It was a dramatic illustration of a phenomenon which is often imperceptible. When we make judgments about whether to trust a person or an organisation, we rely on proxies for the characteristics we’re evaluating. We can’t see the legitimacy of the law firm, but we can see features which we associate with legitimate organisations. The capacity of models to generate text, image and video calls into question many of these assumptions. It could once be assumed that a firm with a website, a domain name and profile pictures of staff was a real organisation with real staff. It used to be so time-consuming to produce these outputs that we could safely assume only a real organisation would be able to do so. Yet now it’s something which a fraudster can produce in a few hours of work.

The same is true for individuals. Until recently if we received an e-mail we would assume it had been written by the sender, unless we knew they had an executive assistant. It’s no longer the case that an e-mail means someone wrote it for themselves or delegated the task to someone else. It’s now possible for commercially available models to answer my e-mail for me, if I was inclined to ask them to. This wholesale outsourcing of e-mail is (thankfully) still a fringe phenomenon but it’s easy to see how it might spread, particularly given how widespread the polishing of e-mails using models has become1. Increasingly, this functionality is just one click away in software like Microsoft Outlook. This automation helps individuals save time but there’s a risk it becomes more difficult to trust the people we’re interacting with as this becomes a widespread practice.

The problem is that we can’t usually know if someone has outsourced their communication to a language model. Yet we start looking for markers of artificiality which may or may not be there. Does the message sound a little strange? Is there something robotic about how they’re communicating? They may have outsourced the writing to a language model, but it could equally mean they’ve e-mailed us in a rush. The perfect spelling and grammar might strike us as suspicious. Does anyone really write like that? Or could it just be that they’ve written the e-mail with care?

Suspicion in the seminar room

It’s particularly problematic when it comes to student work. There’s a particular kind of surface fluency that has become a familiar feature of student assignments: a smoothness to grammar and syntax which feels startlingly artificial if you compare it to examples of student work prior to ChatGPT. Many academic staff imagine this means the student must have entirely outsourced their work to a model. However, it could just as easily be work they’ve produced themselves before using a model to polish the final text. The inevitable tendency to speculate can be deeply corrosive because we can rarely confirm if we are correct about our suspicions.

In late 2025 a mature student at Staffordshire University confronted a lecturer about their use of ChatGPT in an online session which was filmed. A clip from the film was then published by The Guardian. He asserted that “I know these slides are AI-generated, I know that everyone in this meeting knows these slides are AI-generated, I would rather you just scrap these slides… I do not want to be taught by GPT”. I wonder if other academics felt as uncomfortable as I did when watching this video. I’m confident I can justify my use of language models but could I justify it in response to an irate student attacking that use in real time? What if the student didn’t accept the justification I offered? What if the interaction was recorded in order to share it online? I found it hard to watch this video and not be immediately consumed by anxiety.

If we doubt our students and colleagues we must recognise they might in turn be doubting us. I’ve banished em-dashes from my writing because I’m so conscious that readers automatically infer the use of models when they see them. There are countless tiny adjustments we might make to pre-empt the judgements we imagine others might be making of us. When you hear a complaint that reviewer two has ‘obviously’ used ChatGPT or that LinkedIn is now filled with ‘AI slop’ you are nudged to consider if your own peer reviews or social posts might be interpreted this way. If you’re not using models you might worry you’re inadvertently writing in ways that might lead others to assume you are. If you are using them you silently worry you are using them in ways colleagues find problematic. But it’s difficult to seek clarification when everyone seems to be so frustrated by the apparent proliferation of model-generated content.

From moral judgement to diagnosis

The crisis of trust means it’s difficult to talk about a challenge which everyone is facing. This technology is so new we still lack a meaningful sense of what constitutes appropriate and inappropriate use. But how could those norms emerge when we remain locked within an edgy individualism that leaves us reluctant to talk about our use in case others criticise it? Meanwhile the mistrust between staff and students continues to grow. Consider how many academic conversations revolve around the perceived presence of model outputs in student work. Why would we not expect students to be having similar conversations about academics using language models in harmful and careless ways? It’s harder for students to call out staff because of the power differentials involved but the Staffordshire example illustrates that it’s not impossible. Indeed, if this atmosphere of mistrust continues to fester, we should expect these callouts to become more frequent and high profile. If we assume the worst of our students, can we really be surprised if they begin to assume the worst of us?

It’s tempting to react to these changes in moralistic terms. It’s easy to slip into the language of ‘cheating’ and ‘malpractice’ to describe students. But there’s been a pervasive lack of guidance provided to students that would help them understand what is and is not acceptable in this landscape. Where this guidance has been provided it tends to be at such an abstract level that it has little capacity to actually support their decision-making. It doesn’t answer the question “is it ok to do this with a chatbot?” but rather provides principles which they are expected to think about when trying to answer this question for themselves. We have placed our students in an impossible situation and then been surprised when they fail to meet the diffuse and contradictory standards we have imposed on them. The underlying reason for this is that the academic community has failed to reach any meaningful agreement about what those standards should be.

There are certainly uses that seem problematic to pretty much everyone, such as submitting entirely model-generated work for an assignment. But even in these cases we urgently need to recover the capacity to respond diagnostically rather than morally. If students are outsourcing their work to models, then what does this tell us about how we’ve organised teaching and learning? Are there specific factors which might be driving this behaviour for particular groups of students? For example the paid work undertaken by many students often significantly outstrips what is assumed in learning design. Or students might have caring responsibilities. They might be grappling with long commutes because they can’t afford to live near the campus. In many cases language models might be seized upon as a way of making an impossible situation more possible, even if it involves malpractice. The point is not to excuse the behaviour but rather to understand the origins of it. This means rejecting the simplistic explanation that models are causing people to cheat. The more honest reading is that they offer a new way of resolving problems which existed long before the technology arrived2.

A multidimensional crisis of trust

The challenge universities are facing isn’t just how widespread this technology has become since November 2022. If there was agreement about what constituted appropriate and inappropriate use for both staff and students, then we could handle this. The problem arises because everyone knows that many other people are using it, but there’s disagreement about which uses are ok and which uses are not ok. Instead of a reflective practice in which we grapple with what this means for both students and staff, we face what I will argue is a multidimensional crisis of trust. The problem is not simply each relationship in itself but the consequences which accumulate when all these relationships are breaking down simultaneously:

  1. Staff don’t trust students. Too many default to the assumption that students will inevitably use models for academic misconduct. Even when this assumption is warranted it’s unhelpfully treated as a moral rather than a sociological phenomenon. Rather than analysing the conditions which might lead a student to malpractice, such as an unsustainable quantity of paid work, the focus immediately turns to how to identify and hold them responsible. There’s an incipient paranoia in which cheating feels like it’s everywhere, but we cannot be sure who is cheating. There is an accurate recognition that student writing has fundamentally shifted but the explanation for this defaults to the most uncharitable one available. There’s little acknowledgement of the range of ways in which students might use models. This makes it difficult to have conversations with teaching staff who might otherwise be a source of guidance to students.
  2. Students don’t trust staff. There is growing awareness of the degree to which staff are using language models to prepare teaching materials. Jisc’s (2024) survey from the 23/24 academic year found nearly a quarter of academics reporting using models in their teaching. We can expect the number has only increased since then. It’s inevitable that students will develop a similar set of ‘red flags’ to those which staff are prone to looking for in student work. For example, it’s easy to see how the em-dash, popular with many academics, might be seen by students as a tell-tale sign of model use. There’s a legitimate accusation of dual standards in which student use of models is intensely policed while staff seemingly use models with impunity. This could also be framed in terms of value for money, with students resenting the use of language models by staff given the escalating costs of their post-pandemic degree.
  3. Students don’t trust students. Once use of models by students is perceived as ubiquitous there’s an inevitable fear of being penalised if you don’t use the models. It risks creating a toxic climate in which students feel they need to use models in order to compete with their peers. My experience has been that suspicions and accusations of model-related misconduct can be tied up in personal disagreements in extremely complex and corrosive ways. The problem is not so much that students suspect other students of using models problematically but rather that these suspicions reflect and exacerbate existing fault lines within the student community.
  4. Staff don’t trust staff. It’s become commonplace to see academics on LinkedIn or Bluesky complain about a peer review which they suspect has been written by a language model. There are collaborations which fail because academics can’t reach an agreement about the use of chatbots in shared work. I’ve found myself reading an article and strongly suspecting it was entirely written by a model, before being taken aback by how insidious that reaction is. There are existing stresses and strains in relationships between staff which easily get tangled up in concerns about the role of models in higher education.

The breaking of the content/producer link

There’s an underlying shift taking place in the relationship between content and producers. When we encounter something which has been produced (whether it’s an e-mail, a student essay or a slide deck) we imagine we can reliably infer something about the person who has produced it. In the case of the student essay we assume we can make inferences about their learning and knowledge by examining the form and content of the essay. When we encounter a slide deck we assume the person who produced it spent time thinking about the best way to present their ideas to us. The reality is these inferences were always more fragile than we tended to assume: the slide deck could have been adopted from a colleague before being ad-libbed and the essay might have been purchased from an essay mill.

What we’re now seeing is a systemic breakdown of the content/producer link such that distrust in a particular item risks becoming a generalised mistrust in our colleagues and students. I worry we might soon see a tipping point, in which the capacity to place trust in the institution as a site of a common endeavour fatally breaks down. The results of this would be catastrophic for the sector. So much in universities relies on trust and goodwill to keep things operating smoothly. If this breakdown takes place we could expect the secondary systems for managing discord (mitigating circumstances, appeals panels, complaints boards etc.) to be rapidly overwhelmed. Trust breaks down slowly at first, then all at once.

When someone attributes model-based malpractice to another person they are effectively accusing them of behaving inauthentically. It means they are not really showing up. They are not playing by the same rules as the rest of us. When that mutates into a sense that no one is playing by the rules we are entering a truly worrying situation. There’s emerging evidence of the penalties and stigma attached to model use which we need to take seriously (Reif et al. 2025). However, this behavioural literature doesn’t take into account the moral feelings which often accompany such accusations: what it feels like to imagine other people cheating, the sense of grievance accompanying this and how this might play out in terms of already fragile working relationships.

Uneven exposure to suspicion

I would feel able to negotiate a student complaint about use of model generated text. I would explain how I use models to improve the presentation of my ideas rather than to generate those ideas in the first place. But I’m a white male middle-class mid-career academic who studies language models and has students who are taking my classes because they want to learn about models from me. It would be far more difficult for many colleagues to negotiate such a conversation, which is why many academics will have watched that Staffordshire video and thought “god I’m glad that wasn’t me”. This is a situation in which many of us might soon find ourselves, regardless of whether we do indeed use models. Universities urgently need to begin preparing for these interactions.

There were always stresses and strains in these relationships. The pandemic and its adaptations strained these relationships further, changing how we relate to each other within higher education. Once we’ve had the experience of work and study being entirely mediated by screens it’s difficult to return to face-to-face interaction as if nothing had changed. How we work together had already undergone a radical shift which changed how we experienced trust in our organisation. It feels different to work with people you mostly, or entirely, see online than it does to work with people you primarily meet face-to-face. In this sense it couldn’t have been a worse time for any major new problem to have emerged within higher education.

This is why the language of ‘artificial intelligence’ is so unhelpful in its framing of an alien element which is suddenly intruding into already strained interpersonal relationships. Language models are weird enough in their capacity to respond fluently to our messages. They feel like intelligent agents even when we know intellectually they are not. Under these circumstances we need to be intensely careful about how we think and talk about the problems we are confronting. There was so much free-floating anxiety already circulating in the sector and the more it attaches itself to the evocative term ‘artificial intelligence’, the more difficult it will be to work through the practical challenges in the sober and careful ways needed. This is particularly crucial because there are two agendas through which those problems are being defined which have tended to talk past each other in sometimes bad tempered and unhelpful ways: adapting to large language models and integrating large language models.

Adapting to AI

The initial response to language models within universities was driven by a need to adapt to their use by students. It was now possible that a piece of writing seemingly produced by a student was in fact produced by a machine. It couldn’t be assumed that the writing which they had submitted in their name was in fact written by them. This was a significant transformation in how students were perceived even if it was not, strictly speaking, a real change. It was always possible that student work was in fact written by someone else. Indeed that was the entire business model for the essay mills which rapidly grew through the affordances of the web in a multi-sided market which united buyers and sellers. What had changed is that it could now feel likely in many instances.

There is something which feels different about the potential role of language models. They feel more intrusive, alien and ubiquitous than essay mills. Even if technology had turbocharged malpractice through online platforms it didn’t feel like it was something new and threatening. In contrast language models are radically new and unsettlingly fluent. It feels as if they are suddenly everywhere, tempting students to cheat through the immediacy of the responses they offer. Unlike the essay mill that required a substantial payment to compel a human expert to produce an assignment, it is now possible for a student to produce instantaneous responses without even paying for a subscription.

This is new. It is eerie. It is a recipe for paranoia. Our encounters with it in higher education took place against the backdrop of endless claims about ‘artificial intelligence’ and how it would transform every aspect of our lives. This primed everyone teaching in universities to examine their students’ work with a newly critical eye, searching for what were imagined to be the telltale signs of machine writing. To assume everyone might be cheating doesn’t produce a working environment that feels safe or validating. The rapidity with which many academics jumped to that conclusion suggests universities, prone to functioning as what Hall (2014) calls anxiety machines, did not feel safe or validating to their staff to begin with. An institution in which most staff could immediately jump to this conclusion was not an institution that was in a healthy place prior to these developments.

The instinct to constrain

The initial instinct for many institutions was to constrain the technology. To look for systems that could detect the presence of machine writing. To move to formats such as oral exams where a student couldn’t rely on the language model to guide them. To return to face-to-face exams where a student had no choice but to produce their own writing. These impulses were in some ways pedagogically regressive, reverting to practices which the sector had moved away from for largely sound reasons. Introducing a reliance on high-stakes assessments, which students had neither expected nor were prepared for, felt obviously problematic.

For a thinker like Pratschke (2024) these mitigations were a failure to grapple with the educational possibilities of the language models. Instead of recognising that something fundamental had shifted, universities were leaning into outdated processes of assessment. They were gearing up for a detection arms race which could only lead to intensified surveillance pedagogy. Instead of adapting to AI such enthusiasts argued that we must focus on integrating AI. This was the point at which those animated by a longer-term vision and those focused on short-term challenges began to talk past each other. While thoughtful integrationists like Pratschke recognised there were pressing challenges which needed to be addressed, they cautioned that without a longer-term vision of technological integration this problem solving would be narrow and reactive.

However, pedagogically regressive doesn’t necessarily mean wrong. After ChatGPT’s launch I felt dismayed by the calls to revert to in person exams because I felt they imagined we could dispel this technology if only we were sufficiently motivated. It was obvious to me this was impossible. If massively hyped software is made freely available to students3 who engage with their studies through a range of personal devices then how are you going to keep it out? If the biggest corporations in the world are committed to spending billions of dollars on pushing a technology it means we’re going to see a lot of it4. This is a depressing statement about the power which tech giants possess in the 2020s but it doesn’t cease to be accurate simply because it’s dispiriting to recognise. I was deeply suspicious in these initial months that mitigations were motivated by a fundamental denial of the situation in which we found ourselves. Instead of engaging with this technology, working out what it can and can’t do, adaptationists seemed fixated on keeping it out of the university instead.

Why securing assessment still matters

I’ve since realised that the motivations being wrong doesn’t mean the associated measures aren’t necessary. As I argued in Chapter 1, there’s a political need to ensure universities are securing assessment in order to fight off growing attacks upon the sector. There’s a real risk we might see a precipitous collapse of trust in credentials sufficient to overturn the current economic model of higher education. If even a small portion of the predicted AI-driven white-collar job losses emerge over the next decade then the status of degrees will be even more contested than it is today. We simply can’t assume that degrees will retain the baseline level of socioeconomic desirability and cultural allure which they have enjoyed for most of our lifetimes.

Once a majority of students are using LLMs as a routine part of their studies, we have to acknowledge the real and justified doubts this engenders about the status of degrees. While many students use models in thoughtful and responsible ways, the evident fact of their widespread use means we can’t assume a credential necessarily reflects the learning which the institution claims for it. The activity involved in achieving the qualification has potentially changed so much that the fundamental assumptions made about the qualifications are inevitably revisited. It’s essential not only to address these doubts but also to be seen to be addressing them if we want to forestall a significant erosion of trust in higher education. There is the problem itself but also the perception of the problem by external stakeholders.

Even if external stakeholders somehow failed to notice these developments, it’s still something we are required to address for the simple fact that we want to ensure our students are actually learning. The brutal fact underwriting the need to adapt to AI is that we have no meaningful capacity to prohibit our students from using this technology. The diffusion has been so rapid that we are now facing a change which has already happened, even while most institutions remain preoccupied by a transformation they imagine as impending (Bogost 2025). Adapting to AI is necessary if universities want to secure their public legitimacy in the longer term. The problem is that integrating AI is also necessary if we want to preserve the relevance of what we do.

Integrating AI

Over the last few years I’ve occasionally encountered a hostility to my research which I’ve never experienced before. What’s curious about this is that I’ve sometimes been accused of being overly critical and on other occasions I’ve been told I’m insufficiently critical. Indeed on a recent panel an anonymous commentator suggested I must have been taking money from Microsoft, presumably because that was the only explanation they saw for why I wasn’t simply condemning the technology and the firms behind it. I’m not highlighting this to pat myself on the back (though part of me does think I’m doing something right if I’m annoying both sides of a divide) but rather to point to how polarised the debate around ‘artificial intelligence’ has become. I don’t think these critics are wrong to draw attention to the power of big tech firms in higher education. Indeed I’ve spent years researching what I and others term the platform university precisely for this reason5.

We should all be concerned about higher education’s dependence on corporate platforms. Komljenovic et al. (2025: 7) draw attention to the novel dependencies “created by outsourcing capacity development and future planning to private actors” as well as the downstream consequences for teaching, research and public engagement. Investment in (external) platforms becomes the key means through which the university expands its capabilities within core domains of operation. Technology platforms enable universities to perform important tasks but at the cost of locking in dependence upon external suppliers. For example, it’s possible for a university to switch between Learning Management Systems (LMS) if they are willing to bear the costs of doing so. But switching away from the LMS altogether is a more prohibitive undertaking once the organisation has restructured itself around their affordances. This highlights how concerns about dependence need to include not just particular providers but rather entire categories of technology. These are dominated by private providers who are adept at influencing procurement processes, exploiting risk aversion and institutional inertia to ensure open-source alternatives don’t feel viable for university leaders.

It’s easy to cast this in moralistic terms such that you are either for or against technology. There’s certainly an element of this at work in our present debate about language models. I’ve written in the past about the cultural politics of digital technology: how our discussions of technology get pulled towards the utopian and dystopian narratives that litter the cultural landscape (Carrigan 2025: ch 6). When it comes to higher education though, I think something deeper than this is taking place. It’s not just a conflict between competing orientations towards the technology, one boosterish and the other critical, but rather between two strategic logics which roughly correlate to those positions but aren’t reducible to them:

Two strategic logics

  1. Adapting to AI: The rapid uptake of models by students6 creates practical challenges in areas such as assessment integrity, academic writing, student engagement and graduate employability. It is a fact on the ground to which we must respond.
  2. Integrating AI: The innovation made possible by language models creates opportunities for what we do within universities to be transformed, particularly with regards to teaching and learning. It is an emerging condition which we must actively shape.

It’s remarkably easy for advocates of these logics to talk past each other. If you’re preoccupied with adapting to AI, you’re primarily concerned with what’s happening now. If you’re preoccupied with integrating AI, you’re looking towards where we should be going. It would be too simplistic to say the former is present-tense and the latter is future-tense. Adapting to AI means avoiding a future in which these problems get worse, while integrating AI means using present opportunities to realise a desirable future. But they nonetheless entail a different emphasis that creates the risk of approaching the other logic in a slightly baffled way. Why can’t they see how many problems this technology is causing for learners? Why can’t they see that we have to learn to live with this technology? They’re legitimate questions which are far too easily dismissed by the other side in what feels like an increasingly bad-tempered debate. The fact that so much of this crucial professional dialogue is unfolding over social media certainly doesn’t help matters.

Critics of language models are often responding to integration agendas. They see these as dangerous attempts to embed commercial software so tightly into the fabric of the university that we might never remove it. For advocates of AI integration, the political economy seems like a distraction from the core challenge of using this remarkable new technology to enact an overdue transformation of teaching and learning. They can recognise the flaws of actually existing AI labs while still arguing we need to embrace the technology we have. What matters is taking advantage of these new opportunities. There’s an opportunity for richer, more engaging and more personalised forms of learning which the affordances of the technology make possible. This is a serious pedagogical proposition which I engage with at length in the final chapter, even if I remain cautious about weaker versions of the argument which focus on ‘AI skills’.

The workplace argument

A common claim in recent years has been that students will be using models in their workplaces, so we are failing them if we expel the technology from the university system. There’s a naivety to this claim which must be (gently) called out: it assumes current models will develop inexorably over time in exactly the manner AI labs claim. It imagines future workplaces where today’s students will be using what are essentially just more powerful versions of ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini. This has often been framed in terms of ‘prompt engineering’, which is claimed to be the skill of the future that all students will need. This fails to recognise how models are already changing, such that what Mollick (2024a) called a co-intelligence we need to learn to work with has instead become a wizard that acts on our behalf. It’s simply not necessary for most users to prompt carefully in the way they did with earlier models.

In the case of inline automation software such as Copilot 365 and Grammarly, models proactively nudge users into offloading the work of articulation. If you can click a button to “rewrite” or “make longer” without needing to explain what or how you want this to happen, a threshold is crossed in which the substance of the task is being defined by the model rather than the user. There is a worrying trajectory in which the design of consumer-facing software appears to be reducing the need for the user’s thoughtful engagement. This is understandable in the sense that it produces software which is easier to use and more liable to produce productivity gains. But it is a substantial challenge to higher education where the cognitive friction is exactly where the most important work tends to take place.

The notion we must train our students to ‘use AI’ (largely construed in terms of ‘prompting skills’) not only interprets the development of the models in a misleading way, it’s also naive about workplaces themselves. Organisations will be changed radically by the continued development and diffusion of this technology, such that jobs will be changed and jobs will be lost en masse. Anyone who claims in 2026 to be able to define an ‘AI skills’ curriculum which is future-proof is simply not recognising the complexity of what’s coming. However, employers are clearly reporting that they desire ‘AI skills’ in graduates even if attempts to define these are unpersuasive. There are core concepts that can guide us, such as AI literacy7 and ‘21st century skills’ (e.g. creativity, reflection and curiosity) which clearly connect to the affordances of models, even as they continually change. My point is not that we shouldn’t be trying to articulate an AI employability agenda in higher education. It’s rather that developing such an agenda is bewilderingly complex, given the intersection of socioeconomic and technological change, in a manner which too many people working in this space are reluctant to acknowledge.

Credentials, careers and ‘AI skills’

How students perceive credentials could change significantly over the coming years. There is growing evidence of a reduction in entry-level recruitment, even if the causal link between job numbers and the rollout of LLMs remains unclear. If this becomes a sustained trend, we could see a rapid increase in demand for skills imagined to enhance competitiveness in the labour market, even where their actual value is more ambiguous. If ‘AI skills’ come to be seen as a route to employability, that perception will matter, regardless of whether the payoff matches the rhetoric. The credential inflation which has driven the late-stage massification of higher education is itself animated by a diffuse unease about the future: a sense of needing to secure whatever competitive advantage is possible in order to reach a point of relative safety on a sinking ship. If this dynamic intensifies in response to declining employment, particularly where such decline is perceived to result from language models, it has significant implications for higher education.

In this sense, we do need to take the aspiration for ‘AI skills’ seriously, and we should expect this demand to grow over time. This is likely to generate pressure from students for forms of AI integration that extend well beyond the limited purview of mitigation measures. It implies embedding LLMs within curricula, teaching with and about them in context-sensitive and discipline-specific ways, rather than relying on generic provision such as university-wide courses in ‘prompt engineering’ or ‘AI literacy’. This is a complex undertaking with significant demands on staff time and disciplinary expertise. The challenge is not only resourcing this work and keeping provision up to date but ensuring that sufficient knowledge exists within departments in the first place. There is a capacity problem for AI integration which requires significant investment in staff training and development. This is particularly difficult in a rapidly evolving technological landscape where both the capabilities of the models and their institutional significance remain in flux.

Teaching with and about language models

There is no reason why teaching with and about LLMs must involve advocacy of their use. If graduates are encountering a labour market transformed by models, living in a world where the data centre build-out is exacerbating the climate crisis, critical AI scholarship which helps them understand those societal and environmental implications will be imperative. Integration should not be understood as synonymous with endorsement. Unless integration makes space for critical evaluation and limitations on use, then it will not be adequate to the needs and interests of our students. The point is not to abandon critique in order to build ‘AI’ into the fabric of the university; it is rather to ensure critique effectively serves our students and supports them as citizens in the world they are encountering. There is no reason for us to see this as in tension with teaching the practical capabilities which students perceive to be valued in the labour market. The point is that students need both practical capabilities and critical insights to be ready for the world they are graduating into. Indeed, one could plausibly expect that knowing when not to use LLMs, as well as being able to work effectively without them, will be an exceptionally valuable skill in the near future.

The Dilemma Facing Universities

The crisis facing universities requires both adaptation and integration. If we don’t adapt to the diffusion of models, we risk a collapse in the perceived value of degrees. The current system cannot continue unchanged once we have a majority of students routinely using chatbots throughout their studies. It simply upends too many of the assumptions underlying established practices of assessment and conceptions of integrity. If left untended, the problem doesn’t go away. It festers as the everyday life of the university begins to be filled with mundane instances of staff and students suspecting each other of problematic use of models. These implications for trust are potentially existential for the sector, to the extent they erode the legitimacy which underwrites the current funding model. This could be rapidly exacerbated if the crisis is seized upon by external actors pushing towards a defunding of the sector.

If we don’t work towards the integration of models we risk a crisis of relevance. While it’s profoundly difficult to define an ‘AI skills’ agenda that won’t rapidly become obsolete, there is a clear need for teaching methods and curricula to remain in contact with the technological conditions in which graduates will live and work. This doesn’t mean a narrow embrace of technical training, which would itself be a recipe for rapid obsolescence, but rather a more expansive concern with how to live well with technology in a changing world. If we cannot do this, what we offer students will become increasingly unmoored from the practical circumstances they will encounter beyond the university. This is a recipe for irrelevance which could prove as existential for higher education as the crisis of trust, albeit on a longer timescale.

Why each agenda undercuts the other

The problem for universities isn’t that both adaptation and integration are required. The problem is that each strategic logic pushes in a different direction, yet neither can be dispensed with. The mitigation measures involved in adapting to AI take us further away from meaningful integration. How can we enable conversations about how to use LLMs creatively while much of the institution’s energy is devoted to securing large swathes of activity against their influence? Even partial and targeted attempts at prohibition create a situation in which staff and students feel unable to talk about their use. If there’s a sense that use is at least potentially wrong, with no one sure about which practices fall within acceptable boundaries, it becomes extremely difficult for reflective practice to emerge in the manner required for AI integration.

Evidence suggests that teaching staff already find it challenging to have conversations with students about models because they feel beyond their range of expertise in dealing with this technology (Kahn et al. 2025). If an atmosphere of mistrust reigns within the university, these conversations become exceptionally difficult. Yet without them, it is hard to see how AI integration, actively making these systems part of teaching and learning in practice, can take place. Language models may become an object of study within the curriculum, but their use as a learning method and their integration into assessment are substantially hindered. This fundamentally constrains the kind of learning that can occur, as AI becomes something discussed in the abstract rather than engaged with in practice.

Any move towards meaningful AI integration will in turn undercut attempts to adapt to AI. The most ambitious initiatives involve institutional procurement decisions, securing university-wide access to enterprise platforms such as ChatGPT Edu or Microsoft Copilot 365. In doing so, the university establishes a baseline level of access for staff and students, ensuring that everyone can draw on a frontier model within teaching and learning. The problem is that such a move is easily read as a sweeping legitimation of whatever use can be made of these tools. In some cases this simply formalises practices that are already widespread. However, where this introduces new functionality, as with the frictionless automation Copilot embeds within Microsoft Office, it becomes more problematic. How can a university plausibly ask students not to cognitively outsource their thinking when the word processor it provides proactively invites them to do exactly that each time they open a new document? The wave of student and staff protests surrounding early enterprise deals in American universities illustrates the tensions inherent in such moves.

There are significant opportunities offered by AI integration which could transform universities in positive ways. The problem is not the direction of travel but how that change is embedded. A top-down rollout in which the provision of technology precedes the cultural transformation necessary to incorporate it into teaching, research and public engagement risks accelerating use without clarifying its meaning. By its nature this cannot be a rapid process, at least if there is to be a meaningful expectation that these systems will be used in careful and responsible ways. It requires experimentation, space for staff to try things out in relatively safe environments and time for shared understandings to emerge before practices are endorsed and stabilised within routine work. These imperatives sit uneasily with the rule-making and prohibitions that adapting to AI necessitates. In some cases they directly undercut them, licensing experimentation with one hand while seeking to curtail it with the other.

Looking beyond the university

These strategic logics are not inherently incompatible. Under certain conditions, we could move in an orderly way from adapting to AI in the short term, through piecemeal integration in the medium term, towards a more expansive rethinking of the university as the technologies it uses change. The point, however, is that both logics are necessary. This helps explain why advocates of each position so often talk past one another: they are each right in their own terms. The problem is how to pursue them in a coherent sequence, and under what conditions this becomes possible. What I have described as the dilemma is precisely this: the tendency of integrating AI to undercut the conditions for adapting to it, and vice versa.

However, this analysis has so far focused on conditions internal to the university. We also need to situate these dilemmas within the broader macroeconomic context in which AI has acquired such urgency across organisations. It is this context which makes institutional decisions in the near future so fraught, but also where the possibility of a way forward can be found. The likely bursting of the AI bubble renders short-term integration profoundly risky, to the extent that it should be approached with extreme caution. In the next chapter, I explore this political economy in order to clarify these risks, before the final chapter outlines a path forward, strategic under-integration, through which these logics might be coupled together under conditions of profound uncertainty.

About the author

Mark Carrigan

Mark Carrigan is an interdisciplinary academic at The University of Manchester, where he is Associate Dean for Digital Education in the Faculty of Humanities and Senior Lecturer in Education.

There are three strands to his research. The first develops conceptual frameworks for understanding the role of Large Language Models in social life. The second treats higher education as the critical site where their social and cultural dynamics are being worked out in practice, which are encountered through problems that are institutional, cultural and pedagogical as much as they are technical. The Associate Dean role is intimately connected with this second strand, in that his thinking about assessment, integration and institutional strategy is continually tested against the practical work of real-world implementation. The third is psychodynamic, asking what it is that people are doing when they turn to these systems.

Originally trained as a philosopher and sociologist, his work draws on philosophical anthropology, digital sociology, platform studies and psychoanalysis. His long-term theoretical project is to incorporate Lacanian and Bollasian psychoanalysis into Archerian realism to produce a new approach to psychosocial research which is adequate to the existential complexity of our contemporary platformised existence.

Mark has written or edited twelve books. The Platform Learns to Speak (with Milan Sturmer) is forthcoming. Recent books include Platform and Agency: Becoming Who We Are (Routledge, 2025), Generative AI for Academics (Sage, 2024) and Social Media for Academics (Sage, 2019), and he co-edited Building the Post-Pandemic University (Edward Elgar, 2023). He loves writing books and it is the main reason he became an academic.

He serves on the editorial boards of Civic Sociology, the Journal of Digital Social Research and Globalisation, Societies and Education. As joint coordinator of the Critical Realism Network, council member of the International Association for Critical Realism and trustee of the Centre for Critical Realism, he works to support the diffusion and integrity of critical realist scholarship. He is the literary executor for Margaret Archer, and he works to promote engagement with her morphogenetic approach, particularly amongst early career researchers.
