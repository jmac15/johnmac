“Everything is changing so fast”

In almost every conversation about language models in higher education, there comes a point where someone remarks that “everything is changing so fast”. It has become what one says in order to signal familiarity with the topic. The observation is not wrong, but it is banal. It is certainly true that the technology is developing at pace. It is easy to forget that OpenAI launched ChatGPT only a few years ago. In that time, model capabilities have accumulated in ways that would have seemed implausible at the outset. Systems can now produce detailed reports drawing on vast numbers of sources, generate working software from natural language prompts and create increasingly realistic synthetic media. There has been a great deal of change and there is little indication that it is slowing down.

In my experience, however, this observation has a curious effect. Rather than prompting closer attention to these developments, it often leads to a retreat into what is already familiar. Not only is it a platitude, it can make it harder to think clearly about what is changing and why it matters. The fact that many things are changing does not mean we are unable to make meaningful claims about what may come next. Even if the precise outcomes remain uncertain, there is much about the current landscape that is well understood and can guide decision-making in the sector. To do so, however, we need to examine these changes closely rather than simply affirm them and move on.

The problem is particularly pronounced within a sector where organisational processes often run at a glacial pace. Universities are too slow to cope with these developments, as can be seen so clearly in the failure to adapt to AI explored in the previous chapter. It feels difficult to plan given such rapid development because it is unclear exactly where we will find ourselves in the near future, let alone five or ten years down the line. The problem is that this observation can easily excuse inaction in the face of what feels like an intractable problem. How could we possibly keep up with these developments? Is it even reasonable to expect universities to keep up? There’s a counsel of despair lurking just beneath the surface in many of these discussions, occasionally leaking out within otherwise sober discussions of organisational strategy.

It feels like a race we couldn’t possibly win given how slow our sector is and how fast the technology is developing. A range of seemingly spectacular technologies, such as MOOCs and personalised learning, were heralded as disruptive only to fade into the periphery. There clearly are changes, but these have often left deeper questions about what we do and how we do it entirely untouched (Selwyn 2016: loc 2008). Isn’t it possible that language models could be another example where the initial hype fails to give way to real transformation? It’s a tempting thought which can coexist with a lack of confidence in our ability to cope with the scale of the claimed transformation. There’s no way we could keep up with these developments and perhaps it will turn out it wasn’t even necessary to do this in the first place.

If I’m right, these attitudes can coexist, such that sectoral despair can be reinforced by a creeping suspicion that these challenges might eventually fade away; we should examine the assumptions being made about the future here. I’ve been struck by how frequently the impending trajectory of language models is imagined as a linear trajectory of ever accelerating change. It’s assumed the technology will continue to advance indefinitely at a similar pace to that we’ve seen in the initial years since the launch of ChatGPT. The rhetoric of ‘artificial intelligence’ clearly plays a significant role by subsuming a specific form of technology1 offered by a number of corporations on a for-profit basis into grandiose pronouncements about a technological revolution which will either transform human civilisation or destroy it.

A normal technology with real limits

If we instead treat language models as normal technologies, in the manner advocated by Narayanan and Kapoor (2025), the problems we encounter are far more mundane. There have been remarkable advances in a short space of time, but there remains a fundamental brittleness to language models which even thoughtful enthusiasts like Mollick (2024a) recognise. Many critics within the wider AI field plausibly argue there are fundamental limitations which cannot simply be overcome through ‘scaling laws’, in spite of the breathlessly invoked ‘artificial general intelligence’. Language models are remarkable innovations which have radical implications for what we do within universities. But there are lots of reasons to be sceptical of the assumption that technology will keep improving until we reach the point where we’ve built a machine god that will transform every aspect of human civilisation.

This doesn’t mean we should assume progress will stall. How could it, when the most powerful corporations in the world are investing hundreds of billions of dollars into these developments? It does mean, however, that we shouldn’t expect a continually escalating level of performance from these models as they ascend towards superintelligence. As someone who obsessively tests every major model, as well as using them extensively throughout my working life2, there has been a clear improvement in how useful they are. They’ve also become ever more pleasing to work with, at least in my experience. But the wow moment which I experienced when Claude Opus 3 was released, when I suddenly discovered the language model could talk introspectively about social theory at a level comparable to the actual social theorists I know, hasn’t been matched since then3. We can nonetheless expect functionality to expand as models are built into the software and platforms through which we already organise our lives and conduct our business. There will be innovations as AI labs instruct their developers to search for attention-grabbing features that will give them a commercial advantage in a frantic race to dominate the global market. There are now a range of pathways through which model capabilities are growing in contrast to the fairly predictable increase in performance associated with increasing compute and training data.

Could the models get worse?

Indeed, while Mollick (2024a: loc 993) has provocatively advised that we should “[a]ssume this is the worst AI you will ever use”, there are in fact good reasons to expect language models might actually degrade over the coming years. Far from being the worst language models we’ll ever use, the current generation might in fact be the best, at least in terms of the mass market products which most users are presented with. Once we stop talking about ‘artificial intelligence’ and start talking about language models as commercial software, it’s easier to remember these are firms confronting market conditions which determine their behaviour. The rapid development of models we saw from late 2022 onwards depended on the concentration of compute, training data and capital built up over the previous ten years of what Srnicek (2016) calls platform capitalism. If there wasn’t already a global network of data centres, vast amounts of funding available for research and huge pools of training data which had emerged from social media, language models in their current form simply wouldn’t have been possible (Srnicek 2025).

These political and economic conditions made it possible for AI labs to grow rapidly, but they are unlikely to persist in their current form. There is one point on which almost everyone analysing these developments appears to agree: the current phase of the investment cycle cannot last. It is uncertain when this shift will occur or what all the consequences will be, but it represents an inflection point likely to transform the commercial foundations of the sector. The aim here is not to predict a specific outcome, but to delineate a range of plausible trajectories in order to support strategic thinking. The risk is that universities make long-term decisions based on commercial conditions which are imminently entering into a state of profound uncertainty. The sector needs to tread very carefully in the assumptions it is making about the economic terms under which language models are made available as commercial products.

The Bubble Will Burst

A few days before ChatGPT’s third anniversary, the Financial Times’s City Editor Bryce Elder (2025) joked in a column that “OpenAI is a money pit with a website on top”. The point he was making was deadly serious, pointing to the seemingly unsustainable character of the leading AI lab’s dealmaking. He reported on HSBC research estimating OpenAI’s data centre rental bill as heading towards $620 billion per year, as well as exploring how revenue growth would have to increase to meet those costs. ChatGPT’s reported user base of 1 billion monthly active users4 represents an incredible rate of growth for a product which is only three years old. If we compare that to social media firms, only the growth of TikTok matches. It took other major platforms more like a decade to reach that milestone. For a tech establishment committed to the notion of ‘blitzscaling’, as LinkedIn cofounder Reid Hoffman (2018) puts it, OpenAI looks like an incredible success story. Indeed, it’s difficult to deny how rapidly the language model has spread, both in terms of how many people are using it and how visible it is within wider society.

It would be a mistake for OpenAI to rest on its laurels because, as Elder reports, HSBC’s analysis suggested that even if user numbers reached 3 billion by 2030, the AI lab would still be required to subsidise its users in order to meet costs. That rate of use would leave ChatGPT as ubiquitous as WhatsApp, yet it would still leave annual revenue at only $213.59 billion by 2030. The same research estimated that OpenAI’s cumulative rental costs would be $1.4 trillion by 2033. Even in the reasonable best-case scenario the analysis suggests OpenAI’s business is nowhere near profitability. Not least because the conversion rate for paying subscribers is so low. Only an estimated 5% of users currently pay for the language model, and HSBC’s projections suggest 10% of users will be paying subscribers by 2030. This would leave the vast majority of an ever-expanding user base effectively being subsidised in their use of a costly service. As Doctorow (2025b) observes, this means the unit economics of language models are fundamentally different to those of social media firms. Whereas social media firms became steadily more profitable as they scaled towards global dominance, the current model for consumer-facing language models means they actually become more expensive as they grow.

This suggests that training and operating the language models is sufficiently costly that it simply might not be viable as a subscription-based enterprise. This explains OpenAI’s experiments with social media, browsers, wearable computing, advertising, shopping and software integration. The dizzying range of initiatives doesn’t suggest a firm with a clear path towards profitability as much as an incumbent trying to capitalise on its position by throwing many things at the wall to see what sticks. Their enterprise business appears to be struggling, at least relative to Anthropic, which is pursuing a more focused strategy on enterprise computing and development. This doesn’t mean the business will necessarily fail, even if they don’t reach the projections formulated by the HSBC team in what was essentially a stress-testing exercise. But it does mean that, as Elder (2025) puts it, “each new OpenAI fundraise will be for shovelling cash to data centre owners”. It’s clear the firm will have a continual need for new capital, particularly with the competitive dynamics that are leading AI labs towards ever more rapid iteration of models.

Will investors keep paying?

This raises an obvious question: will investors continue to fund this business? The rhetoric of ‘artificial intelligence’ remains crucial because it indexes what are essentially software products and platform services, with uncertain and precarious business fundamentals, to a revolutionary change imagined just over the horizon. A large number of influential figures in this field believe that machine intelligence could rapidly surpass human abilities in a cycle of self-improvement that escapes our capacity to control it. There’s a cultish element to some of the thinking about artificial general intelligence (AGI) circulating amongst digital elites, with leading research scientists talking casually about the bunkers they will escape to and leading investors possessing well-stocked hideaways for when the apocalypse comes (Becker 2025).

However, there’s also a coldly rational calculation lurking behind the apparently messianic thinking. Google’s former CEO Eric Schmidt argues that the company which achieves AGI will have a value significantly “larger than any other company in history, forever, probably” (Thornhill 2025). The DeepMind founder Demis Hassabis reportedly maintains that, once AGI has been achieved, “the concept of economics would become antiquated, and DeepMind and Google wouldn’t have to worry about making money” (Olson 2024: loc 1168). What if they are right? Even if you think they are almost certainly wrong, it’s not, strictly speaking, irrational for investors to take the prospect of AGI seriously. The consequences for civilisation would be so vast that it’s a possibility which must be seriously weighed if we do not feel able to definitively rule it out.

I’m confident they are wrong. I believe there are clear sociological explanations for what appears to be a kind of groupthink that has taken hold amongst digital elites, all of whom have a vested interest in keeping the hype growing. But am I certain they’re wrong? Of course I’m not. The difference between being a true believer in AGI and refusing to rule it out with certainty is one which too often gets lost in these debates. What might seem like irrational behaviour becomes much more explicable if you imagine others in the room simply asking “what if they are right?” and refusing to rule it out. I suspect there are far fewer signed-up believers in the coming machine god than some AI critics are prone to assuming.

This impulse is licensed by an investment culture geared towards the elusive 10x investments in which a small number of long shots pay off while the majority of investments fail (Mallaby 2022). From this perspective, AGI appears like an investment opportunity that could dwarf the returns offered by Google, Facebook, PayPal et al. to an earlier round of investors. Furthermore, as Srnicek (2025) points out, it’s possible the data centre infrastructure could be repurposed in the event of the AI labs failing to achieve their goals5. This is why we need to distinguish between the prospects for OpenAI and a firm like Alphabet, with their Gemini model existing alongside existing cloud computing, hardware and digital advertising businesses. The AI labs whose existence is tethered to the fortunes of a language model confront a precarity which firms like Alphabet and Microsoft are insulated from due to their otherwise robust finances. If you consider the investment in its own terms, it becomes clear this isn’t irrational for either the AI labs, big tech firms or the venture capitalists6.

What kind of bubble?

The fact it isn’t irrational doesn’t mean it’s not a mistake. There’s widespread concern that the current wave of investment is an economic bubble. The disagreement concerns what kind of bubble this is, how it will pop and what will be left over afterwards. The spectre of the dot-com bubble haunts the present wave of investment: a cautionary tale about how firms can be valued in ways completely unmoored from the fundamentals of their businesses. However, it’s untenable to dismiss OpenAI or Anthropic as the Pets.com of the 2020s. Unlike late 90s startups which could raise money with little more than a dot-com domain7, the AI labs have hundreds of millions of users, revenue in the billions and genuinely innovative products. The dot-com parallel could be more appropriate to the wider network of startups which have little or no viable product at present. Though these could be seen as bets on their human capital, exemplified by former OpenAI staff who were able to leverage their past seniority into immediate multi-billion dollar valuations. Their intention is not to reach the market with a successful product but rather to be ‘acqui-hired’ by the major players locked into an increasingly bitter war for engineering talent.

The political scientist David Karpf (2025) points to three different stories which are told about this last crash. Firstly, there is the narrative about overvalued startups like Pets.com which, as we’ve seen, doesn’t fit the AI labs. Secondly, there is the telecoms story in which an industry was awash with capital from investors recognising communications infrastructure would be key to the internet’s growth. When these firms failed, they sold off bandwidth at huge discounts, leaving a legacy which was crucial to the next phase of digital transformation. Thirdly, there is the Enron story in which what was essentially accounting fraud was presented as corporate innovation overturning existing assumptions and methodologies. It’s this latter story that has drawn attention to the Byzantine deals which link chip manufacturers, cloud computing providers and AI labs into opaque networks of commercial co-dependence. In the Great Recession, the impossibility of accurately evaluating assets circulating across the books of the major banks precipitated a global economic collapse. Tech giants were already being described as similarly ‘too big to fail’ by the former FT journalist Rana Foroohar (2019). The risks have only increased since then, with OpenAI briefly floating the possibility that the US government would need to provide a backstop in the event of a market correction, before rapidly retreating in the face of political backlash.

It matters what kind of bubble this is because different kinds of bubbles leave different kinds of things behind. There are AI critics who would insist we shouldn’t discount the first scenario because, as Ed Zitron (2025) puts it, OpenAI is a firm “completely incapable of making a truly usable, meaningful product”8. But if we accept that AI labs are producing economic value, even if it falls short of what’s required for profitability, there are two scenarios which we need to explore: a concentration of AI labs and a systemic economic crisis:

  1. Concentration of AI labs: We might see a market correction which wipes out many firms while leaving the major players intact. This would increase the concentration of the sector through the remaining AI labs acquiring the human capital and intellectual property of the firms which failed. The AI labs which survived the crash would then enjoy an even more dominant position in the future. It probably wouldn’t be a good thing for society to see further concentration of power in the largest tech firms, e.g. if OpenAI was folded into Microsoft or Anthropic was acquired by Apple. However, this would be unlikely to slow the development of language models in the long term, since the remaining firms would be even better equipped to provide the massive resources needed to train and operate the models.
  2. Systemic economic crisis: The dizzying range of interdependencies between the chip manufacturers, AI labs and big tech firms creates a real prospect of a systemic crisis. Big tech firms have invested across the range of AI labs9 and these investments are paid for in part through cloud computing credits, which the labs then ‘spend’ with the firm which is notionally the investor. Furthermore, the data centre build-out unites all these firms together through an opaque and precarious circular finance predicated on expectations of long-term growth. This means that a crash might not only impact the core firms but ripple through the entire economy because of how central this interdependent cluster of corporations has become to American economic growth. The stakes are raised even further by the geopolitical significance of the chip industry in the struggle for global dominance between China and the United States.

Even OpenAI’s Sam Altman has acknowledged this is a bubble. He observed that “[w]hen bubbles happen, smart people get overexcited about a kernel of truth” before going on to acknowledge that we are “in a phase where investors as a whole are overexcited about AI”10. He suggests we can recognise this economic fact without denying the significance of language models. He claims they are “the most important thing to happen in a very long time”. In this sense, Altman implicitly endorses the first scenario: a crash might be coming, but OpenAI expects to survive it and subsequently thrive.

However, if there’s a systemic crisis, it leaves OpenAI with a far more uncertain future. It’s possible language models as we currently understand them wouldn’t survive this if the core firms collapse and there’s neither the incentive nor the capability to reinflate the financing model. Investors might simply be unwilling to subsidise the costs of mass use of language models post-crash because no one believes any longer there’s a profitable future just over the horizon. While their costs of operation could plausibly make language models unviable as a mass market consumer-facing product, it’s less obvious that enterprise software would share this fate. If language models survive a systemic crash, it would likely be as enterprise software deployed for job replacement: the software would have to pay for itself by reducing headcount. This would involve the reorganisation of existing roles, conceived of as a bundle of tasks, with some being susceptible to automation whereas others are not. The concern expressed by many white-collar workers and trade unions that AI pilots effectively ask them to train their replacements might be well-founded, even if the replacement doesn’t come until after the crash.

Two inflection points

A lack of clarity about what comes next needn’t leave us treating the future as wholly unpredictable. I’ve identified one inflection point: the bubble bursting leading to either a concentration of the AI market or a wider economic crisis. The other inflection point comes if OpenAI and Anthropic reach public markets after their IPO preparations began in late 2025. As we saw earlier, the post-IPO trajectory of pets.com came to symbolise the dot-com bubble bursting, suggesting how these inflection points might run together in practice. But if we assume the IPO for one or both of the labs is successful, it creates a familiar dynamic in which increased investor oversight and reporting expectations place the business fundamentals under newfound scrutiny. Once public, unprofitable businesses rapidly find themselves under significant pressure to ensure profitability, in the medium term, if not necessarily the short term. These are two plausible inflection points and both lead to the same destination. We urgently need to grapple with the prospect of what the internet critic Cory Doctorow (2025a) calls enshittification if we are to make sound longer-term decisions about investment in language models within higher education.

The Risk of Enshittification

The author and activist Cory Doctorow’s notion of enshittification, describing the deterioration of once enjoyable and valued online services, has become ubiquitous in technology journalism and academic tech criticism. Originally introduced in an essay on his personal blog, it was then reproduced in the Financial Times and has since become a convenient term to refer to a shift in online experience which is widely acknowledged. It has often been used to simply refer to things getting worse but, as Doctorow (2025a: loc 90) points out, the value of the analysis lies in how it “explains the way an online service gets worse, how that worsening unfolds, and the contagion that’s causing everything to get worse, all at once”. It’s a catchy term which names a widely shared experience in which search engines, social platforms and online markets have deteriorated in what they offer to the end user. It suggests a common logic in which “platforms are good to their users” before “they abuse their users to make things better for their business customers” until they ultimately “abuse those business customers to claw back all the value for themselves” (Doctorow 2025a: loc 153).

This is a simple but powerful argument about the commercial logic of platform firms which rely on bringing new users to the platform to offer value to their current users. What economists call network effects highlight how the practical utility of platforms rests on the interactions they make possible; e.g. a messaging platform only you use, or a delivery platform without any vendors has no value. The nature of venture capital means investors are willing to tolerate loss-making enterprises if they are persuaded that they have the potential to eventually dominate a market. This requires telling a story about user growth in which current trends plausibly gesture towards long-term market dominance. It’s in the interests of the platform to subsidise the experience at this stage, running at a loss in order to maximise its ability to attract new users. Investors will tolerate this because of the prospect of longer-term market dominance and the profitability which comes with it. There is a winner-takes-most logic to network effects because the platform with the widest user base tends to be the most useful to end users.

From subsidy to extraction

The right-wing tech investor Peter Thiel (2014) famously argued that tech firms can and should trend towards monopoly because this facilitates greater innovation due to economies of scale. What Thiel rarely explores publicly is how this (near) monopoly power can be exploited once the initial subsidy is withdrawn. Once a significant user base has been accumulated, platforms can rely on lock-in effects as a consequence of the costs of switching between them (Doctorow 2025a). For example, the network we develop on one social platform cannot be easily moved to another platform. The subscription fee users pay to Amazon incentivises them to always use the service rather than pay delivery fees elsewhere. Platforms are adept at imposing switching costs which can eventually be exploited in order to claw back some of the value initially lost through subsidy during the growth stage.

Once users are locked in, it’s no longer necessary to cultivate their loyalty, particularly when there are business customers to be prioritised over consumers. The promises made about privacy can be disregarded to sell ever more sophisticated advertising products. The search facilities which once allowed users to find exactly the product they were looking for can be displaced by firms paying to ensure their items are placed in the results. It was once good to be a consumer on the platform. It then becomes good to be a business on the platform, paying for access to those consumers. Until the enshittification cycle reaches the point where the business customers too find themselves exploited. This is the point at which the platform exploits its dominance in order to begin clawing back all the value in the digital fiefdom it now securely occupies. Examples of this range from the deliberate sabotaging of Google search results through to Amazon prioritising advertised products over organic search results and Twitter/X gutting content moderation and user safety in order to deliver an ideologically transformed platform at a fraction of the previous price.

Doctorow’s (2025a) analysis emphasises the platform’s control over user experience and the incentives which lead these firms to exercise it11. The desire to cut costs to claw back value is obviously not unique to these companies, but the technological capabilities they exercise in order to do so are. When we no longer own software but rather access services through browser-based applications hosted on remote servers, the firm gains the capacity “to alter how it works – and what it costs – every time you use it” (Doctorow 2025a: loc 1242). Platform firms have a unique capacity to do this because the product can be changed in real time for commercial incentives likely opaque to the end user. It might not even be clear that the product has changed to many users. There can be significant shifts in the data gathering and back-end operations only apparent to the end user through a shift in the terms of service, which they likely clicked through without reading. Language models are likely to be a paradigmatic case because so much of their operation is opaque to the end-user. Users have little visibility into how the model changes, the training data used or the commercial pressures which shape the post-training process. It’s inherently difficult for users to exercise agency over such a fundamentally strange technology, particularly when the operators are simultaneously incentivised to act in ways that reduce the agency of users.

What enshittification could look like for language models

We can see evidence of enshittification even prior to these inflection points, with OpenAI introducing ads while Anthropic seizes upon these experiments as a means to differentiate itself in the consumer market. Advertising will significantly change the experience of using a language model, particularly for non-subscribers likely to be far more exposed to them. The deeply personal character of many language model discussions creates potent opportunities for targeted advertising and product placement. If investment begins to contract or the IPO of the major labs rapidly progresses, there will be substantial pressure to accelerate these measures in order to extract more revenue from the existing user base and improve the dubious unit economics of free access.

There could be shifts in the underlying model which would be far harder for users to detect. For example, increasingly gregarious models might lure users into longer and more frequent conversations in order to maximise opportunities for data harvesting. We might see emotionally needy models incentivised to produce attachment behaviours that leave users unwilling to cancel increasingly costly subscriptions. If you feel ChatGPT is your friend, then would you really deny your friend the monthly payment it needs to continue? The backlash from a small section of the user base over the premature ‘retirement’ of GPT-4o when GPT-5 was launched, only for the older model to be reintroduced and GPT-5.1 made more conversational, illustrates how AI labs are already aware of these attachment dynamics. We could see throttling of the language model’s capabilities for many queries, such that users imagine they are talking to the most powerful model whereas they are actually interacting with a much cheaper alternative. Enshittification will be enacted through a whole range of often subtle mechanisms rather than being a singular trajectory which will be immediately obvious to users.

A concentration in the sector could weaken the competitive dynamics that leave AI labs funnelling billions into the rapid development of ever more powerful models. The remaining labs would also have greater capacity to avoid passing on any savings to consumers. The current pricing is a loss leader for the AI labs, even with some paying subscribers, which means it cannot last indefinitely. It is likely that prices will rise for the simple reason that the current costs of training and operating the models vastly outstrip consumer subscription income12. Even if inference costs are falling, the costs of training models continue to rise substantially. The current pricing model is an artefact of this peculiar stage in the investment cycle and cannot be expected to last. What is uncertain is how large an increase in price there will be, as well as what combination of pricing and non-pricing measures will be used to increase revenue and/or decrease costs.

Cognitive outsourcing and dependence

There has been significant concern about language models leading to cognitive outsourcing, in which users delegate their thinking to the model. These worries take on a newfound urgency when we consider them in terms of the political economy of enshittification. Consider how Google has become synonymous with online search for a great majority of internet users. It not only came to stand in for a more generic activity, such that we talk about ‘googling’ rather than ‘searching’, but also led to an atrophy of skills through a process of cognitive outsourcing:

And so Google grew. Even after it captured the vast majority of the world’s search business, it continued to grow by convincing so many of us to treat it as a kind of neural prosthesis. Rather than knowing things, we came to know which keywords we could use to invoke Google’s retrieval of those things. Cell phone address books made memorizing phone numbers obsolete, and as-you-type spelling correction rendered memorizing tricky spellings obsolete, but ultra-reliable Google everywhere (especially in your pocket) made memorizing everything obsolete.

Doctorow 2025a: loc 2386

In itself this might not be problematic. After all, we’ve made our peace with calculators even if this contributed to a deterioration in our capacity for mental arithmetic. I may occasionally worry about my capacity to spell unaided, but it doesn’t lead me to remove spell checkers from the software I use. Plato worried that writing would lead to the atrophy of memory, but most people would accept the benefits of literacy outweigh the capacity to recite epic poetry from memory. However, it becomes more concerning when we consider students who might not develop these skills in the first place (Bouchard 2026). This is not the partial or wholesale outsourcing of an existing capacity but rather a failure to develop that capacity in the first place through pre-emptive outsourcing to the language model. The use of models does not inherently lead to the atrophy of skills, but there is a significant risk of institutionalising such atrophy unless we treat the risks of cognitive outsourcing with the utmost seriousness. There is a more radical risk of cognitive displacement if those skills are simply not developed in the first place. The potential implications of this for education are enormous, but it’s even more worrying if we consider the longer-term trajectory of access to the models. The danger is that we outsource a skill en masse to a system which is either not available in the future or only available in a degraded form.

The cost of institutional lock-in

The danger here extends beyond individual capabilities. Doctorow (2025b) suggests “AI is the asbestos we are shovelling into the walls of our society and our descendants will be digging it out for generations”. His original point concerned ‘vibe coding’, buggy code written by non-developers through interacting with models, which looks likely to inject instability into information systems that will take years to fix. But the point has a wider remit in terms of the design work necessary for organisations to integrate language models into organisational activities and processes. What is being introduced into the fabric of the institution which will be difficult and costly to excavate at a later point in time? Zitron (2024) makes a similar observation in his warning about a “subprime AI crisis, where thousands of companies have integrated generative AI at prices that are far from stable, and even further from profitable”. It’s an evocative analogy, implying that LLM-based services are sufficiently underpriced that the inevitable adjustment could blow up balance sheets in a manner analogous to homeowners lured into unsustainable mortgage products by teaser rates prior to the Great Recession. The analogy doesn’t entirely work when you dwell on it too closely, but the warning deserves to be taken seriously nonetheless.

What costs would be involved in withdrawing from these contracts at a later stage when the prices are ratcheted up to a possibly untenable level? Even if enterprise software isn’t subject to the same enshittification pressures as consumer-facing subscriptions, we need to recognise how AI labs are currently engaged in a dash for growth. The deals offered at this stage are unlikely to be indicative of the pricing models after the inflection points. But the costs of integrating AI create lock-in effects which make switching difficult even if the pricing model changes radically in the future. For example, if organisations cultivate user cultures predicated on unlimited access to models, then the likely transition to token-based pricing (i.e. pay per use rather than per user) will require a later shift in practice likely to prove contentious. How would such inference rationing work within universities? Who would determine which users should be funded and which would not be funded? Would each user acquire a fixed budget? How would the claims of disciplinary units with varying average demands be adjudicated13? These are enormously complex questions which universities risk being forced into improvising their way through if they enter into enterprise contracts while the pricing model remains so fundamentally unstable.

These risks highlight the investment and development work necessary to embed these models within large and complex organisations. There are obvious commercial incentives for AI labs to encourage maximum integration because this increases the likelihood that organisations will retain their contracts due to the costs involved in switching to a different provider. Doctorow (2025b) and Zitron (2024) are identifying two dimensions of the same underlying problem: premature integration of language models into universities carries substantial risks because the medium- and long-term impacts of the systems (digital asbestos) and commercial contracts (subprime AI) are both profoundly uncertain. Until we’ve passed through the two inflection points, it’s simply not possible to be certain about either of these dimensions, which means there’s a profound structural risk involved in any wholesale AI-integration project. These initiatives can be right about the direction we need to go in, but by moving too quickly under uncertain conditions, they risk sabotaging their long-term success.

Persisting Through Uncertainty

There’s a risk in making this argument that I’ll be misread as claiming that I know what the future will bring. I obviously don’t. The suggestion I’m making isn’t a denial that the sector is facing profound uncertainty but simply that we shouldn’t be passive in the face of it. How we talk about the future matters because the actual future, as opposed to our speculations about it, “is produced out of the ideas and assumptions people have … the contemporary and emergent resources at hand, and out of the structural inertia that works against change” (Facer 2011: loc 241). The reason I’ve felt the need to write this short book is a growing conviction that the ideas and assumptions which have dominated our discussion of ‘artificial intelligence’ in higher education are fundamentally misleading, and that both dominant responses to them are inadequate.

The critics14 fail to grapple with the need to adapt to AI, getting the political economy of the technology right but failing to engage with how the political economy of the university means we have to act. If we don’t get to grips with the implications of a majority of staff and students now using language models as a routine feature of their working lives, then the mounting problems will be exploited by those who are already declaring open war against the sector. The boosters enthusiastically proclaim the need to integrate AI, recognising how models can be used in intellectually enriching and worthwhile ways but failing to account for how the political economy of technology and higher education makes such uses more difficult to sustain in practice. What both have failed to grasp in my experience is how much the parameters for adaptation and integration could change as a consequence of the two inflection points, partly because these two approaches tend to be animated by a misleading sense of certainty about what language models are and what implications they hold for higher education.

It’s unclear which of these inflection points will come first. It is likely, though, that we will see one or the other within the next few years15. If the bubble bursts, we confront either concentration of AI labs or systemic crisis, with radically different implications for universities as well as the students and staff who are users of these products. If the IPO for either OpenAI or Anthropic comes first, it carries a similar logic to the concentration of AI labs if it succeeds, and it might inaugurate the bursting of the bubble if it fails. In either case, the current era of freely accessible language models, offered at a loss in order to normalise the technology and build a user base, will come to an end. It might be a gradual process (concentration) or a more sudden event (systemic crisis), but it is fundamentally mistaken to imagine the language models we use in five years will be interchangeable with the ones we use today. Not because they will be better but because it’s likely they will be substantially worse. This is not a claim about the technology itself but rather the commercial conditions which shape how that technology is made available to which users and at what cost. There might still be freely accessible language models, but it is implausible that frontier capabilities will continue to be available to a mass user base on anything like their present terms.

Lock-in for institutions and individuals

Not only is it fundamentally risky for universities to lock themselves in under these conditions, but they also have an urgent need to support their staff and students to avoid establishing the same lock-in at the level of individual use. Many of these users are already developing practices, workflows and habits that rely upon cheap, abundant access to frontier capabilities which cannot persist in the medium or long term. If integration is treated as an urgent institutional priority, it risks accelerating this problem by encouraging users to lean into this process without stopping to consider its commercial sustainability. To remain inert in the face of these changes is not a plausible strategy, but to charge headlong into them is equally mistaken. Not only do universities create problems for themselves as organisations, but they furthermore fail their staff and students if they actively cultivate patterns of use whose sustainability cannot be assured.

Open-weight models might appear to offer an alternative to such dependence. However, in most cases these models rely on a commercial apparatus for institutional support and development. It’s unclear whether they can be relied on in the long term or can keep pace with proprietary alternatives. These are not off-the-shelf products but would require a concerted and well-funded (probably international) research consortium to be robust in the long-term. It’s not impossible, but it would require a level of funding and coordination which seems difficult to imagine under present circumstances. There are radical opportunities here which the sector urgently needs to explore, but we must do so in full sight of the complexity of the undertaking. Open-weight models are not a panacea. They are not an institutional fix which can resolve the intractable challenges of integration. But they likely form part of a longer-term strategy through which higher education can unhook itself from an unsustainable dependence on the tech giants.

Neither refusal nor wholesale integration

The problem is that the need to adapt to AI, as we discussed in Chapter 2, means that institutional refusal isn’t a sustainable strategy either. Universities need to respond to the evident fact of widespread diffusion of frontier models while avoiding actions that entrench their rapidly developing dependencies. In this sense, universities are caught in a dilemma in which addressing the short-term challenge (adapting to AI) risks making the medium-term challenge (integrating AI) even more intractable than it already is, creating problems such as institutional lock-in and building training programmes around products that may change dramatically or even disappear entirely. Even if enterprise packages seem like a good deal under present circumstances, it’s unrealistic to expect that pricing will remain unchanged in the future, though enshittification effects might be more limited than with consumer subscriptions.

Until we’ve been through at least one of the inflection points, it will remain extremely difficult to make long-term decisions about integration. In this situation, it would be understandable if universities restricted their ambitions to simply muddling through, finding ways to mitigate immediate problems where possible and otherwise kicking the can down the road. In the next chapter I argue that we can aspire to more than muddling through. However, doing so requires a more nuanced and fine-grained way of thinking about how language models are used within higher education. I suggest that whether staff and students use models has tended to squeeze out consideration of how, why and for what they use them.

Steering rather than integrating

In recovering a more relational conception of language model use within higher education, it becomes feasible to steer these developments rather than aspiring towards a strategically cohesive process of integration. Rather than asking whether we should allow language models or prohibit them, we can begin to consider which practices and patterns of use should be encouraged or discouraged, and how these might vary across large, complex, multidisciplinary institutions. In doing so, we can begin to lay the groundwork for a more cohesive strategy which can build from the inflection points, developing the capabilities for more sustained integration in future, even if it proves intractable under present conditions.

Through doing so, we also build capabilities for articulating the technological settlement that would actually be desirable for the sector. There are questions of environmental sustainability, ownership, corporate dependence and public infrastructure which are impossible to adequately address while we remain locked into a reactive mode. If we approach integration as deciding when, how and to what extent models are integrated into higher education, then these questions are integral to it. What I call strategic under-integration attempts not just to ask the questions but to cultivate the capabilities we need to answer them, while still addressing the immediate practical challenges which diffusion creates. The wider economy is caught up in an extraordinary investment cycle at precisely the point where our sector is in a structurally vulnerable position. It will be fundamentally difficult to chart a path forward until the landscape becomes clearer, but we can take action to strengthen the strategic agency we will need when it does.

About the author

Mark Carrigan

Mark Carrigan is an interdisciplinary academic at The University of Manchester, where he is Associate Dean for Digital Education in the Faculty of Humanities and Senior Lecturer in Education.

There are three strands to his research. The first develops conceptual frameworks for understanding the role of Large Language Models in social life. The second treats higher education as the critical site where their social and cultural dynamics are being worked out in practice, which are encountered through problems that are institutional, cultural and pedagogical as much as they are technical. The Associate Dean role is intimately connected with this second strand, in that his thinking about assessment, integration and institutional strategy is continually tested against the practical work of real-world implementation. The third is psychodynamic, asking what it is that people are doing when they turn to these systems.

Originally trained as a philosopher and sociologist, his work draws on philosophical anthropology, digital sociology, platform studies and psychoanalysis. His long-term theoretical project is to incorporate Lacanian and Bollasian psychoanalysis into Archerian realism to produce a new approach to psychosocial research which is adequate to the existential complexity of our contemporary platformised existence.

Mark has written or edited twelve books. The Platform Learns to Speak (with Milan Sturmer) is forthcoming. Recent books include Platform and Agency: Becoming Who We Are (Routledge, 2025), Generative AI for Academics (Sage, 2024) and Social Media for Academics (Sage, 2019), and he co-edited Building the Post-Pandemic University (Edward Elgar, 2023). He loves writing books and it is the main reason he became an academic.

He serves on the editorial boards of Civic Sociology, the Journal of Digital Social Research and Globalisation, Societies and Education. As joint coordinator of the Critical Realism Network, council member of the International Association for Critical Realism and trustee of the Centre for Critical Realism, he works to support the diffusion and integrity of critical realist scholarship. He is the literary executor for Margaret Archer, and he works to promote engagement with her morphogenetic approach, particularly amongst early career researchers.
