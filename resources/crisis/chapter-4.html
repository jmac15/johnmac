What does it mean to ‘use’ a language model?

What does it mean when we say a student uses an LLM? For many staff, the answer seems so obvious that it barely merits consideration. Using a model means not doing a task yourself. It means outsourcing your thinking in order to replace your own work with the model’s output. This assumes a zero-sum relationship between what the person does and what the model does, such that using a model means forgoing one’s own activity in a manner a responsible institution ought to police. This is sometimes articulated in a strident and explicit way, but more often it lurks beneath the surface as an unexamined assumption when we talk about issues such as malpractice.

This is a compelling intuition, but it fails to recognise the range of ways in which models can be used in practice. Using an LLM isn’t like moving from handwriting to a word processor, such that the mere presence of the technology signifies something fundamental has shifted. There are certainly better or worse modes of writing with a word processor. As I’m writing this chapter, each paragraph fills with blue underlines (style and grammar) and red underlines (spelling) as a real-time contribution to my writing. The red underlines are largely correct, though in one case the software has misidentified the word I was trying to spell, such that accepting it without review would introduce a mistake. The blue underlines are a mixed bag which require careful review, occasionally flagging needless verbosity but often changing the meaning of sentences in an obviously problematic way. There are nuances of academic expression which Word’s style and grammar checker is curiously oblivious to. The software scaffolds the editing process, but it remains one I am driving as an author. In an important sense, the word processor gets out of the way during writing until you actively draw on its functionality to refine your work.

Using a word processor doesn’t mean the software is doing the writing in any meaningful sense. Language models, in contrast, can do the writing for us if we want them to. It is clear, though, that many people who use models regularly do not actually want them to do this for them. This is a desire which we need to take seriously, particularly in our students. The earliest iterations of GPT could do little more than complete the text they were presented with; a neat trick with limited real-world application. This is why AI labs invested in discerning a user’s intentions through analysing how users judged different kinds of responses the model provided1. This enabled them to adapt fluently to whatever the user brought to the interaction. Whereas the word processor entails a shift in how we write, it does not significantly reshape what we write or why. In contrast, models change every aspect of the writing process because of the range of ways in which they can be used. It is possible to outsource the writing process entirely to the model with little more than a sentence of instruction. The risk of inadvertent outsourcing grows as the models become ever more capable of inferring what the user might want based on what they have shared.

Thinking with AI, or a substitute for thought?

I spent 2024 writing a book about LLMs and academic writing, which I ultimately abandoned when Claude began to offer me whole sections to complete my incomplete draft. I realised that with a 60% finished manuscript, the model could mimic my style sufficiently that it could plausibly finish the book for me. Would anyone know? Would that matter? In the end I felt so uncomfortable with the prospect that I abandoned the project, instead posting it as a series of essays on my blog. Not only can we outsource our intellectual work to LLMs, but the temptation to do so can arise even for those who have reflected at length on why this would be corrosive. I seriously considered letting Claude finish the book for me, and I spent the subsequent two years reflecting on that experience and the risk it highlights. The problem is that there will always be another deadline in higher education, an occasion on which it might feel acceptable to outsource just this once. Even if you reject the idea in principle, the temptation can creep in as a practical response to circumstances.

The potential uses of LLMs range from actively using models to enrich existing intellectual processes through to outsourcing those processes entirely: what I described in Carrigan (2024) as thinking with AI or using AI as a substitute for thought. The notion that ‘thinking with AI’ is possible still attracts scepticism, but it is demonstrably possible to use models to elaborate, evaluate or refine your ideas. They can function as a research assistant, sparring partner, supervisor or whatever role provides intellectual value in a given situation. These interactions are not a substitute for intellectual exchange with other people, but they can be a remarkably valuable supplement to it. Even when a task is partially outsourced, it does not necessarily follow that thinking has ceased. For example, I used ChatGPT Pro to help edit my last book, but I had a clear sense of what I wanted to achieve and carefully reviewed the outputs. It becomes wholesale outsourcing when the model is expected to plan or define the task itself. In such cases, you are no longer thinking with the model but using it as a substitute for thought.

A spectrum of use

Any use of LLMs can be positioned on this spectrum. The problem is that it is far easier to slide from one end to the other than many users acknowledge. If we want to maintain intellectual standards, we need to understand this drift towards outsourcing and how to resist it. This requires clarity about what the spectrum actually entails. Jackson (2025) offers a useful model, distinguishing between four degrees of LLM use in learning:

  • Human-centric learning involves no LLM use. Until recently, all learning fell into this category, but it has become newly significant in an environment where models are ubiquitous. It may involve a deliberate commitment to low- or no-tech pedagogy.
  • Machine-in-the-loop learning is human-led. The model is used for specific functions, such as topic exploration or feedback, within a workflow directed by the user.
  • Human-in-the-loop learning is machine-led2. The workflow is organised through the model, with the human making decisions within an interaction shaped by it, for example, generating and iteratively refining a draft.
  • LLM-centric learning entails minimal human involvement, typically involving a single prompt and response, and is unlikely to produce complex understanding.

The impact on learning depends on how LLMs are used. There is no impact on human-centric learning because no model is involved. Machine-in-the-loop keeps the human in control, allowing the model to support learning. Human-in-the-loop involves a partial displacement of control, which can be productive but carries risks. LLM-centric use, by definition, precludes meaningful learning, even if it may be desirable in limited circumstances. Obviously, in reality, this is an extremely fluid matter, in which a user might move up and down the spectrum countless times while undertaking a single task.

Learning to ‘shift left’

What is valuable about Jackson’s approach is not the typology itself but his suggestion that movement along this spectrum can be a guiding pedagogical principle. We should design learning activities that encourage students to “shift left”, using LLM-generated outputs as a starting point for developing human-led understanding and application (Jackson 2025: 11). The point is not simply to categorise use, but to support staff and students in sustaining their agency in how they use language models. Cognitive outsourcing is not a single error but a habit, one which requires countervailing habits to resist it. The disposition to “shift left” is an important habit which counteracts a tendency towards outsourcing without asking users to avoid language models entirely. It is one which we need to develop ourselves and to help our students develop. This might mean cultivating a considered scepticism of software which hinders shifting left at the level of the underlying interface. It is far easier to do this in a dialogical interaction with a chatbot than it is in using software which automatically summarises a document for you or enables you to turn loose notes into a presentation with a single click.

How do we cultivate that disposition in practice? In part, this is a matter for learning design which I’ll discuss in the next section. But dispositions are formed through reflection, relationships and norms rather than through learning design alone. It requires a relational infrastructure to provide the conditions in which organisations can offer a sense of what is appropriate conduct and what is not. To develop this, we need to move beyond the parallel agendas of adapting to AI and integrating AI by focusing on building relational capacity, which is the focus of the final section.

What Does This Mean for Learning Design?

Instead of framing a single act of using a model as intrinsically positive or negative, we need to focus on sustained patterns of use over time. This means taking the opportunities for expansive pedagogy seriously while remaining attentive to the risks. The range, flexibility and interactivity of language models offer genuinely radical possibilities, but integrating these into teaching and learning is a more precarious undertaking than many advocates of AI integration acknowledge.

The promise of expansive LLM pedagogy

Consider, for example, Pratschke’s (2024) vision of LLMs as enabling more engaging and personalised teaching by leveraging the affordances of models for content generation and discussion. There is a genuinely compelling vision here: a pedagogy organised around interaction with models, where generated outputs become the object of critical reflection (Pratschke 2024: loc 2918). There is certainly value in all students undertaking this work at some point during their degree. The question is how widespread such a practice should be. Is it a means through which students develop critical AI literacy or a pervasive feature of pedagogical practice? The risk is that model outputs begin to substitute for human outputs if we pursue this too enthusiastically.

Even with a layer of critical reflection, a profound substitution has already taken place if model-generated outputs become a routine feature of teaching and learning. This approach risks stabilising learning at a particular point in the spectrum in a way that may be harmful over time. The problem is not learning to do this but rather reorganising learning more broadly around it. What Pratschke terms generativism explicitly advocates “the idea of collaboration with GAI in every type of learning activity” (Pratschke 2024: loc 2185). While this emphasises active and constructivist learning, it still gives the LLM a dominant place in the student’s learning experience (Pratschke 2024: loc 2231). In practice, this implies a shift towards human-in-the-loop pedagogy, which is certainly preferable to LLM-centric learning but still carries significant risks.

This is not a transition that could or should be undertaken all at once, but it does point towards a direction of travel. The instincts behind strong AI integration are not mistaken, but they require a counterbalance if they are to avoid reinforcing the very dynamics they seek to overcome. If we are integrating with one hand, we need to be protecting other learning outcomes from integration with the other. This might involve incorporating ‘low tech, no tech pedagogy’3 into our learning design, using creative methods (such as game-based learning, creative production or eco-pedagogies) alongside the models we are bringing into the classroom. Even if aspects of AI integration make for attractive pedagogy, we need to consider how its consequences accumulate over time if learning becomes predominantly mediated through interaction with the model. Occasional reliance on ChatGPT is unlikely to undermine learning. However, if the practice of question-asking itself is developed primarily through interaction with the model, then something more profound may be at risk. We need to develop capabilities before we can outsource them.

In some cases this might be possible through interaction with the model; in many cases it will not be. If we commit to embedding models into every aspect of learning, then it becomes difficult to distinguish between cases where learning with the model is possible and cases where the use of the model displaces learning. It’s possible to learn with models, but it’s implausible that everything can be learned, in every circumstance, with them. If we assume it’s impossible to learn with models, then we let our students down and risk irrelevance in a world where models are ubiquitous. If we commit to maximal integration, then we lose the opportunity to identify and safeguard those capabilities which are difficult or impossible to learn with models. The costs of cognitive outsourcing accumulate through sustained patterns of use rather than one-off interactions (Hansen 2024). This is why learning design must encourage Jackson’s (2025) “shift left”, ensuring that movement into LLM-reliance does not remain entrenched there.

The political economy of pedagogy

The bigger problem with Pratschke’s (2024) advocacy lies at the level of political economy. If pedagogy becomes embedded in interaction with the machine, what happens when that machine is no longer as accessible, engaging or helpful as it currently is? It takes an early phase in the rollout of a technology and advocates rebuilding education around an accurate and plausible account of its affordances, without considering how those affordances may change over time or be supplemented by constraints that reshape what we can do with it. In doing so, it risks reproducing the very dynamic that Pratschke, rightly, warns against. While she’s right to warn against LLMs being used “to revert to outdated models of delivery”, she fails to consider how her own approach creates the groundwork for “increasing automation in education” (Pratschke 2024: loc 2231). If we see AI integration as the insertion of LLMs into every aspect of teaching and learning, we create the conditions in which instructors come to appear as costly irrelevances within an otherwise machinic pedagogy.

This does not mean they will be dispensed with immediately or everywhere. Rather, the human role is likely to be gradually eroded within a transformed political economy. By definition, human-in-the-loop instruction will tend to be cheaper than human-centric instruction. This creates the conditions in which pedagogical arguments for human-in-the-loop instruction blur into economic arguments for it. If “outdated models of delivery” are understood as the result of insufficiently enlightened learning design, it becomes easy to present more technologically intensive approaches as the obvious solution, justified in pedagogical terms but driven by economic incentives. Pratschke (2024), to her credit, points to exactly these concerns but does so in a way that leaves the causal relationship underexplored. The risk of “increasing automation in education” follows logically from generativism rather than being a failure of it. We shouldn’t dismiss Pratschke’s pedagogical argument on this basis, but we should qualify endorsement of it by considering what the institutional implications would be if this agenda were pursued in full.

The problem is that models of delivery cannot be understood in isolation from their political economy. What Weller (2020) calls the “infinite lecture hall” model of MOOCs was pushed so forcefully in the early 2010s because it appeared to free prestigious universities from the economics of scarcity. If one Harvard professor could teach the world’s philosophy students, then what was effectively a luxury good could be distributed globally. MOOCs did offer real benefits, most obviously flexibility for lifelong learners, but to explain their rise and fall in purely pedagogical terms is to ignore the commercial pressures shaping them. The same is true of large language models. While learning designers may not be able to transform the context in which they work, this does not justify ignoring how that context shapes and is shaped by learning design over time. In this sense, my argument is less a critique of Pratschke (2024) than a call for approaches to pedagogy and learning design which are institutionally and economically sensitive about their own context.

Holding promise and risk together

This is particularly important given the economic uncertainty outlined in the previous chapter. It cannot be assumed that current systems will remain stable in terms of capability, reliability or pricing. At the same time, they cannot be ignored given their widespread use by staff and students. Configuring learning entirely around currently available systems is therefore risky. It risks deepening platform lock-in, incentivising automation as the human role is gradually eroded under resource constraints, and leaving students anchored in human-in-the-loop learning when the aim should be to “shift left”, making LLM use one component within a broader learning process. This is the only way to address the pedagogical challenges posed by existing use while supporting the development of meaningful practice. We can support students in learning to use language models creatively and carefully, without risking them coming to be dependent on commercial software with an uncertain future.

We can learn from Pratschke’s advocacy of expansive LLM-enabled pedagogy while remaining attentive to its longer-term consequences. As an account of present possibilities, there is much to commend it. My concern is that the conditions which make this approach attractive will be undercut as its downstream effects accumulate. The risk is not immediate automation but iterative change: not the removal of academics, but the gradual scaling back of their role through a series of apparently pragmatic decisions. The most important question is not whether models are used, but how. The aspiration towards human-centric and machine-in-the-loop learning is not a rejection of LLMs but a pragmatic attempt to ensure they support, rather than displace, the learning process.

From Whether to How

If we start from the boosterish assumption that LLM use always involves thinking, it becomes difficult to see how easily it can slide into careless use. Earlier models required a degree of reflection to produce adequate outputs through what was often termed ‘prompt engineering’. While this was always an inflated notion, some care was clearly necessary to generate usable results. Ill-formed prompts tended to produce generic or incoherent outputs, meaning that worthwhile responses required a clear sense of what one was trying to do. In this sense, reflection was built into the process. It meant that careless outsourcing was relatively easy to detect when generic writing began to proliferate in the months following ChatGPT’s launch.

This is why I was relatively relaxed about the implications of early models. Careless use tended to produce poor outputs that were easy to spot. In retrospect, that confidence was misplaced given the significant shift in model capabilities. They have transformed into what Mollick (2025) calls “wizards”, where “for an increasing range of complex tasks, you get an amazing and sophisticated output in response to a vague request”. It is now possible to get good enough outputs with ill-thought-out prompts. What a user gets from the model will still be improved substantially by thinking about what they are asking it. But the model will now meet you halfway in a manner which removes the cognitive discipline implied by this constraint. This shift reflects changes in training, with reinforcement learning increasingly supplemented by highly qualified domain experts in areas such as medicine, law and science. As a result, models are better able to interpret user intent, reducing the need for users to specify and refine their requests. It is now much easier to slip into careless outsourcing because clarity of intention is no longer required to produce convincing outputs.

If we start from the assumption that LLM use always involves cognitive outsourcing, it becomes difficult to say what is being lost. If we assume all use is lazy, careless or otherwise undesirable, it limits our capacity to engage pedagogically with the evident fact of their widespread use by staff and students. Many critics seem to argue that language model use will necessarily be detrimental to learning. It is still unclear to me how this claim could possibly be justified. Examined more closely, this position reveals a form of technological determinism that is often disavowed in such critiques.

If language models are always detrimental, in all circumstances, then there must be some constant feature of the technology which explains this outcome. If, however, we accept that LLMs can be used in ways that meaningfully contribute to learning, the question shifts to the conditions under which those uses can emerge and be sustained. This reframes the problem away from the technology itself and towards the contexts in which teaching and learning take place, and how these enable or constrain different forms of use. Increasingly, I share the pessimism of the critics, but I locate responsibility in the interaction between our institutions and the technology, rather than attributing it entirely to the technology itself. It is not that language models cannot be used in engaged and reflective ways, but that such uses are difficult to embed and scale under existing conditions.

Looking at what people actually do

To understand this, we need to look more closely at what staff and students are actually doing with language models. This means examining how and why they move through Jackson’s (2025) spectrum, and how we might cultivate a culture in which they instinctively “shift left”. The problem is not human-in-the-loop or LLM-centric use in itself, but the risk of remaining there. Recognising the range of possible uses raises an obvious question: why do some people use models in one way rather than another? The assessment panic is so corrosive because it encourages a moralised rather than diagnostic approach to this question. It promotes the assumption that all use is fundamentally problematic, leading many staff to infer that signs of superficial polishing necessarily indicate deeper cognitive outsourcing.

This obscures the variability of student use and makes it harder to offer meaningful pedagogical guidance about which practices to encourage and which to discourage. Some uses are relatively straightforward to evaluate: using a model to explain a concept will often be beneficial, even if it may displace other forms of engagement, whereas copying and pasting outputs into assessed work without editing is difficult to justify. The bulk of student use, however, falls between these extremes, making judgement necessarily contextual. Activities such as idea generation, summarisation or structuring an assignment depend on how they are undertaken, and in relation to what the student is doing and why.

If the breakdown of trust leaves students unwilling to discuss their use openly, we can neither offer guidance nor develop the experience needed to know what guidance to offer. It also limits our ability to have meaningful conversations with colleagues about what such guidance should look like within particular disciplines. When professional use remains opaque, it becomes difficult to sustain shared understandings of LLM pedagogy or to cultivate a culture in which staff can reflect openly on their own practices. Unless we can learn to have open and thoughtful conversations about these issues, it is difficult to see how higher education can develop the shared understanding needed to cope with the practical challenges that continue to accumulate.

Asking why students outsource

Even when we agree that a particular use is problematic, we need to ask why students are engaging in it. What does it say about higher education if a substantial number of students are using technology to evade learning? What pressures, such as paid work, caring responsibilities or long commutes, might be driving this behaviour? In many cases, we are seeing problems made newly visible through the lens of LLMs rather than created by them. These technologies undoubtedly introduce new challenges and exacerbate existing ones, but without a more fine-grained way of thinking and talking about their use, it is difficult to respond effectively. The technology in this sense is a mirror (Vallor 2024). If we do not like what we see in that reflection, we are mistaken if we blame the technology alone.

The Case for Strategic Under-Integration

There is no good ‘AI strategy’ under these conditions. Recognising this cannot license inaction in the face of mounting challenges. Action is necessary, and it must be strategic. However, there is no single strategy capable of navigating the complexity universities now confront. What we confront are wicked problems in the classical sense of the term, such that there are only better or worse options involving trade-offs (Corbin et al. 2025). What I call strategic under-integration is something more than muddling through, but less than a roadmap that attempts to move institutions from their current predicament to a clearly defined AI-infused future. Any attempt to impose a singular vision in this context fails to grapple with both the instability of the technology and the crisis affecting the sector itself. The risk is that an ‘AI strategy’, in the orthodox sense of the term, precludes agility in the face of repeated waves of technological and institutional upheaval (Corbin, Sharpe and Dawson 2026).

This is not an argument for inaction, but for a different mode of action: one that combines adaptation and integration while recognising the risks of premature commitment. In other words, we should not be trying to do all of this at once. This is not simply a problem of quantity, in which entire portfolios will need to be reviewed and revised. That would be a problem of triage, which is difficult but tractable. The deeper difficulty is that the criteria through which this review can take place themselves require continual review, as the technology changes and our understanding of it develops. This in turn relies upon institutional capacities which are inconsistently developed at best. Furthermore, there cannot be a one-size-fits-all approach across large multidisciplinary institutions, because the better or worse approach will vary depending on the disciplinary context.

The point is to keep building institutional capacity while the ground shifts beneath our feet. This is what has often been missed by both agendas. The organisation itself needs to change while this is taking place, connecting professional development to the networks through which academics cluster into disciplinary and problem-based communities. Adaptation should not be reduced to assessment integrity, AI literacy and academic malpractice. Integration should not be reduced to assessment reform, curriculum redesign and institutional platforms. Both approaches are preoccupied with systems and overlook the relationships through which these problems are encountered and ultimately addressed.

The difficulty is not only that these are hard problems, but that they are being faced in conditions that leave universities less well equipped to respond than at any point in recent decades. We lack the organisational capacity to grapple with technological change in ways that produce norms and standards capable of generating shared understanding. This problem is particularly pronounced in relation to large language models, but it is by no means unique to them. There is an institutional rebuilding required here, strengthening the relationships and collective capacities through which universities make sense of change, even while it feels, at least at points, that the ship is sinking.

The only way to unlock this is to develop the relational infrastructure needed to navigate technological change collectively. The fact that no long-term decision is viable under current conditions makes this our only progressive option. Without it, a narrow adaptation agenda will dominate through mitigation measures that fail to address the underlying challenges. If the argument I have made over the preceding chapters is correct, it would be a mistake to treat present-tense policy formulations as solutions to what is fundamentally a longer-term problem. What we need instead is a combination of short-term adaptation, medium-term integration, and a longer-term project of building the capacity to cope with ongoing change:

Three agendas, three timescales

  1. Adapting to AI (short-term): recognise the rapid diffusion of LLMs among staff and students; secure at least some assessment for each programme4; provide clear guidance on expectations in learning and assessment; ensure these expectations are discussed clearly and consistently with students; respond to infractions diagnostically rather than moralistically.
  2. Integrating AI (medium-term): teach with and about LLMs in discipline-specific ways; develop assignments in which validity does not depend on excluding LLMs5; experiment with LLM pedagogy while avoiding lock-in; develop AI literacy as an embedded practice; design learning that incorporates Jackson’s (2025) “shift left” so that we do not remain in human-in-the-loop learning; support the development of disciplinary exemplars of integration; use these to encourage the diffusion of best practice.
  3. Building capacity (longer-term): properly resource professional development; train and equip LLM-ready learning designers; build staff learning networks; create staff and student forums for deliberation; rebuild faculty governance of information technology; use the technology to support these processes.

Pulling in different directions

The existing debate on LLMs in higher education has been dominated by the first two agendas. Each is correct in its own terms. We urgently need to adapt to the evident fact of widespread language model use within universities. To continue without this adaptation fails our students and creates the conditions for a precipitous decline in trust in degrees and in each other. There is a parallel need to integrate LLMs as objects of knowledge and as methods of teaching and learning, given their rapid impact on the world beyond the university. Without this, we risk failing our students and undermining the relevance and value of our credentials. Each of these agendas requires capacities that universities, particularly in their current post-pandemic form, do not yet possess. All three agendas are essential, yet the third has been largely overlooked in the clash between the first two.

The problem is that we cannot easily pursue these agendas simultaneously. The practical demands of adapting to AI make it harder to integrate it, encouraging measures that are pedagogically regressive. Reverting to exams, oral assessment and analogue methods carries a diffuse air of prohibition that makes it difficult to foster creative engagement with LLMs. If there is a general sense that use is potentially wrong, it discourages the conversations we need about how these systems are being used and whether those uses are appropriate.

Conversely, integrating AI makes adaptation more difficult by encouraging uptake and making it harder to articulate negative judgements about its use by staff and students. Providing institutional access makes it less plausible to discourage or prohibit certain forms of use. Without integration, adaptation becomes a permanent state of firefighting. But without adaptation, integration leaves universities unable to address the urgent challenges created by rapid diffusion. We need both, but sequencing is the challenge. What can hold them together is the development of relational capacity to cope with ongoing change.

Building the Capability to Cope with Change

Over these four chapters, I have explored how change at the level of individuals (staff and students) is crucial for understanding change at the organisational level of the university. For many readers, the challenges at each level may seem familiar: at the individual level, questions of digital competence, access, ethics and future prospects; at the organisational level, issues of assessment integrity, trust in credentials, employability and inequality. A substantial literature has already emerged on each of these in the three years since ChatGPT brought LLMs into everyday use.

My focus has been on linking these levels together, showing how LLMs reshape the capacity of people within universities to trust one another (Chapter 2). The problem cannot be understood in narrowly technological or pedagogical terms. It requires attention to how organisational life is being transformed by the rapid diffusion of LLMs among staff and students, the forces driving that diffusion, and the wider political economy shaping both higher education and the technology itself (Chapter 3). It also requires a more fine-grained account of how these changes are lived in practice, and how staff and students respond to them in their everyday work (Chapter 4).

If we see workflow as the sequence of technologically enabled tasks through which individuals enact complex projects, it becomes clear that models have radical implications for how we work together within universities (Knowles 2024). Few tasks are beyond the reach of these systems in principle, creating an almost infinite range of possible transformations as different users incorporate them in different ways. Even subtle differences can destabilise established patterns of work more profoundly than earlier technologies such as social media, MOOCs or the LMS. The problem is that access to consumer-facing models has expanded far more rapidly than the mechanisms of professional deliberation needed to govern their use. When individual use spreads without shared norms or standards, it produces a chaotic and uncertain user culture in which it is unclear what is appropriate and what is not.

Lessons from social media

We have seen a similar dynamic with the early use of social media in academia, where many academics became daily users without clear professional standards for engaging diverse audiences (Carrigan 2019b). This blurred the boundaries between public and private, as well as between the ‘inside’ and ‘outside’ of the university, in what Healy (2017) described as “latently public” conditions (Bacevic 2019). Over time, professional norms emerged, and institutions developed ways of regulating this space, albeit imperfectly. For many in higher education, social media has since receded into the background as a less prominent object of professional concern. It is no longer experienced as a professional problem in the manner it was for much of the 2010s. Even if this normalisation carries its own set of problems, it highlights how technologies move from being novel and disruptive features of our environment to being taken-for-granted habits and infrastructures of academic life.

Incorporating new technologies into higher education is not just a challenge for individuals seeking to realise their potential and avoid their pitfalls. It is also a challenge for reflective practice, given the remarkable flexibility of LLMs across the full range of digital activity. Whereas social media primarily affects activities such as networking and public engagement, language models have implications across the entire spectrum of academic work. The real difficulty lies in how these individual changes interact, as workflows shift in uneven and unpredictable ways. This destabilises the shared understandings that make coordinated work possible, contributing to the breakdown of trust explored in the second chapter. In this sense, the capacity for collective responses to change is undermined by the very changes that demand them, eroding the tacit structures through which complex organisations coordinate their activity.

The next wave

In the later stages of writing, there were rumblings of another impending wave of technological change. Commentators pointed to rapid advances in LLM-based coding agents, suggesting that systems capable of autonomously building and deploying software in response to natural language prompts may soon become widespread. While such claims are often overstated, recent developments indicate that these systems are becoming significantly more capable. It is increasingly plausible that we will see further waves of transformation comparable to that which followed the launch of ChatGPT, even if the trajectory and political economy of these changes remain uncertain. The rapid growth of wearable AI is another development with the potential to significantly impact higher education. Corbin, Sharpe and Dawson (2026) plausibly argue that student use of these devices has the potential to fatally undermine attempts to secure assessment.

The point is not that a particular prediction will come to pass, but that the changes will not stop. Technological development is unfolding alongside longer-term transformations in how universities are organised, funded and governed (Collini 2012). What we are seeing is an acceleration of these processes in a feedback loop with technological change. LLMs are not the first technological challenge to the university, but they are unusually comprehensive in their implications. This is the point at which we can no longer treat such developments as technical matters to be administratively managed. If we fail to grapple with them now, we risk being overwhelmed by successive waves of change to the point where the capacity for strategic response begins to break down. How we act now matters for the changes which are coming, not just the ones we are currently grappling with.

Who governs technology in universities?

Who governs information technology within universities? This is not a new question. Over a decade ago, McCluskey and Winter (2012: loc 300) warned that “if faculty members do not control information technology, the information technology department will control the faculty members … This is a question about the future of the university. It is a binary question. It is that simple”. It is not that simple, but the concern is well placed. The technologies we use are not merely technical infrastructure; they shape every aspect of what we do and how we do it. The increasing complexity and resource intensity of this infrastructure has been accompanied by centralisation, placing key decisions beyond meaningful oversight by those responsible for the everyday operations of the university. If those who are delivering teaching and research are not involved in decisions made about the provision of technology, these will tend to be poorer decisions for it. The problem is epistemological as well as democratic. If academic and professional services staff on the ground are not incorporated into decision-making, those decisions will be deprived of situated knowledge about the actual challenges encountered in delivering core functions within the university.

The growing dominance of platform models, in which software is provided as a service by external providers, further entrenches this dynamic by outsourcing core operations. Where platforms are effectively controlled by external actors, there are intrinsic limits to institutional governance, a condition we might describe as the platform university (Carrigan 2019a; Carrigan et al. 2023). Over the last decade, universities have increasingly come to function as providers of infrastructural services enabling distributed work. This shift was both relied upon and accelerated during the pandemic, reshaping participation and belonging within the institution. The governance of information technology has never been strongly influenced by frontline staff, but that influence has diminished further under these conditions.

There is an urgent need to rebuild governance in the face of LLMs. The Russell Group (2023) principles, released soon after the launch of ChatGPT, recognised that “appropriate uses of generative AI tools are likely to differ between academic disciplines” and that institutions should therefore “encourage academic departments to apply institution-wide policies within their own context”. This reflects a crucial point: there can be no set of central principles adequate to the complexity of the situations academic staff face when adapting to AI. These challenges are too variable and context-specific to be resolved at the level of general guidance. This creates a need for professional judgement at the level of individual academics, teaching teams and departments, not simply to interpret policy but to elaborate it in practice. Without this, principles remain abstract, leaving unanswered what they mean in specific settings: a research project, a doctoral student, a large undergraduate lecture or a small postgraduate seminar. Attempts to impose uniform rules across such varied contexts inevitably produce gaps and ambiguities and risk exacerbating existing confusion and mistrust.

Adapting to AI therefore depends on enabling these local understandings to emerge. The Russell Group (2023) also recognises this, emphasising that “engagement and dialogue between academic staff and students” must be “regular and ongoing” to keep pace with these developments. The problem is that this work was largely imagined as taking place through subject associations already stretched by the pandemic, rather than within institutions themselves. What would it mean to treat this as a core institutional responsibility? A number of elements would need to interlock in a sustained and coordinated way over time if we hope to build sufficient relational capacity to cope with accelerating technological change:

Six ways to build relational capacity

  1. Build staff learning networks. The flexibility of LLMs means they can be used in a wide range of ways across disciplines, producing highly variable practices within large institutions. There is an urgent need to identify and circulate emerging practices and issues from real contexts. Not all staff will engage actively, so mechanisms are needed to support both active and passive participation. For example, a regular newsletter combining case studies and resources can enable broad engagement while providing opportunities for more active contribution. The workload pressures which most staff are subject to in the contemporary university represent a huge barrier to organisational learning. It is imperative that we design around these to meet the needs of staff who would like to engage but cannot feasibly prioritise these engagements.
  2. Create staff and student forums for deliberation. Learning networks enable practices to emerge, but they are less suited to evaluating them. There is a need for forums in which staff and students can consider not just how LLMs are used, but whether they should be used in particular ways. These should not be confined to specialists but embedded within existing structures such as programme teams and departmental meetings to support wider participation. There is a need to identify and establish a working consensus on these issues within disciplinary communities to avoid the normative chaos concerning ‘AI’ which we currently confront within the sector.
  3. Build distributed governance of information technology. Academic and professional services staff involved in the everyday operations of the university should have meaningful influence over decisions about the provision and regulation of LLMs. This must be balanced against the need for institutional coordination, given the risk of slowing already constrained organisations. Governance should therefore follow a principle of subsidiarity, devolving decisions where possible while establishing central principles that can be adapted locally. Without such influence, technical decisions will shape teaching and research without adequate academic input. A central role for staff on the ground remains essential, particularly in areas with significant pedagogical implications. However, this influence must be focused and operationally meaningful, rather than extending to all aspects of technical infrastructure. The challenge is to balance centralisation and decentralisation in a way that supports both coordination and contextual judgement.
  4. Resource professional development adequately. Without time and capacity, none of this is possible. Even where staff recognise the importance of development, it will be crowded out by immediate demands unless institutions make space for it. This requires resisting short-term efficiencies that generate longer-term problems. Gains from tools such as Copilot must be protected rather than converted into increased workload. The argument that LLMs will free up staff for high value work is seductive but without a strategy for protecting this productivity dividend it will inevitably be clawed back. This could easily become the means through which time for professional development is made available if institutions are willing to commit to that protection.
  5. Train and equip LLM-ready learning designers. Learning designers will play a central role in integrating LLMs into teaching and learning. They need practical experience with models, as well as a clear understanding of how to design for movement across Jackson’s (2025) spectrum, particularly the cultivation of “shift left” practices. If they are embedded within disciplinary communities, this provides a foundation through which trust can build over the course of sustained collaboration. While some academics might be able and willing to drive the integration work themselves, they are likely to be in a minority. Learning designers have a crucial role to play in supporting the academics who lack the time and expertise to do so independently, as well as gradually expanding the ranks of academics capable of leading this work themselves.
  6. Deploy the technology to support these processes. LLMs themselves can support institutional learning and development, particularly when resources are constrained. They can enhance existing processes and enable new forms of professional development. However, they must be used carefully given the tendency for efficiency gains to be reclaimed. For example, internally developed chatbots with knowledge bases grounded in policy and pedagogy could be a continuous source of support which supplements learning design and educational technology teams. However, it is essential that those teams are involved in designing and implementing these solutions. If automated forms of support can operate as a triaging mechanism for human expertise, the relationship can be complementary. If they are drawn upon to reduce human staffing, the automated support will be a poor substitute for the required professional relationships.

These are not solutions to the problem. They are a means of building the relational capacity needed to grapple with a problem that will continue to grow and mutate over time. Indeed, the assumption that these developments permit straightforward solutions is precisely what this proposal seeks to reject. This is a fundamental shift in the technological environment that will require universities to change how they operate internally. What I am offering here are starting points for what strategic under-integration looks like in practice. What matters is maintaining forward movement while resisting the siren song of vendors offering one-size-fits-all enterprise solutions. These are not solutions but strategies for proceeding purposefully in the absence of solutions. It is an attempt to carve out a pragmatic middle-ground between muddling through reactively and immediately integrating language models so deeply into the fabric of the university that we lose the ability to remove them when the commercial landscape inevitably changes.

When technology becomes invisible

A curious feature of technology is that we tend to notice it only when it is new or when it breaks. We have seen this repeatedly, with e-mail, search engines and social media moving from disruptive innovations to background infrastructure. This is particularly pronounced for ‘artificial intelligence’, a category so overloaded with meaning and expectation. As one of the founders of the field, John McCarthy, reportedly remarked, “as soon as it works, no one calls it AI anymore”. Once a technology becomes a mundane feature of routine work, it fades into the background, losing the almost magical character this terminology imbues it with. LLMs are currently in their visible phase, where their capabilities and limitations are still widely discussed. The controversies surrounding their use in academic life mean they cannot yet recede into the background. Instead, they intrude into everyday practice, forcing reflection in ways that sustain those controversies. Even the most enthusiastic users will recognise the experience of encountering an AI-infused feature they neither need nor want. Each of these interruptions illustrates how the technology has not yet become ordinary, even as it comes to be ever more embedded in the routine infrastructure of academic work.

It is clear, however, that this will not last. Consider meeting transcripts in Microsoft Teams, which have moved from a remarkable innovation to a routine feature of university life. Or the novelty of online meetings, which were new for many during the pandemic but are now a mundane aspect of how we work together. The same will be true of language models within a few years. This transition will not happen all at once. Some features will fade into the background while others remain prominent, and it will unfold unevenly across different users and contexts. The window of opportunity we currently have to shape how these technologies integrate into our universities will eventually close: questions about how models should be used may come to resemble debates about e-mail: widely recognised as problematic but largely left to individuals to manage in isolation. By that point, the technology will be too deeply embedded to allow for easy reversal or meaningful redirection.

Conclusion: what is to be done?

Across these four chapters, I have sought to answer a simple question: what is to be done? The difficulty for the sector is that there is no single answer, because the challenge exceeds any one problem that can be resolved before we move on. This is a fundamental shift in the technological infrastructure through which the university, and wider society, operates. But this does not mean we can do nothing. At present, the window of visibility is open. This is an issue that everyone within the university is compelled to think about and form opinions on. That will not last. The window will close.

In the short term, we need to adapt to AI before it does. We need to resolve the most pressing questions of trust before habits form and understandings become entrenched. In the medium term, we need to begin integrating AI, recognising that doing so judiciously will involve trial and error. Move too fast, and we lock in assumptions that will quickly become outdated; move too slowly, and we risk failing our students and losing relevance. The longer-term task is to build the institutional capacity to cope with a continual, if uneven, process of technological change. This is what connects the other agendas: enabling us to respond to present challenges while preparing for those to come. If we do not begin this work while the window of visibility remains open, we will miss the opportunity to embed the practices and structures we will depend on in the years ahead. It’s not too late to get this right, but time is short.

About the author

Mark Carrigan

Mark Carrigan is an interdisciplinary academic at The University of Manchester, where he is Associate Dean for Digital Education in the Faculty of Humanities and Senior Lecturer in Education.

There are three strands to his research. The first develops conceptual frameworks for understanding the role of Large Language Models in social life. The second treats higher education as the critical site where their social and cultural dynamics are being worked out in practice, which are encountered through problems that are institutional, cultural and pedagogical as much as they are technical. The Associate Dean role is intimately connected with this second strand, in that his thinking about assessment, integration and institutional strategy is continually tested against the practical work of real-world implementation. The third is psychodynamic, asking what it is that people are doing when they turn to these systems.

Originally trained as a philosopher and sociologist, his work draws on philosophical anthropology, digital sociology, platform studies and psychoanalysis. His long-term theoretical project is to incorporate Lacanian and Bollasian psychoanalysis into Archerian realism to produce a new approach to psychosocial research which is adequate to the existential complexity of our contemporary platformised existence.

Mark has written or edited twelve books. The Platform Learns to Speak (with Milan Sturmer) is forthcoming. Recent books include Platform and Agency: Becoming Who We Are (Routledge, 2025), Generative AI for Academics (Sage, 2024) and Social Media for Academics (Sage, 2019), and he co-edited Building the Post-Pandemic University (Edward Elgar, 2023). He loves writing books and it is the main reason he became an academic.

He serves on the editorial boards of Civic Sociology, the Journal of Digital Social Research and Globalisation, Societies and Education. As joint coordinator of the Critical Realism Network, council member of the International Association for Critical Realism and trustee of the Centre for Critical Realism, he works to support the diffusion and integrity of critical realist scholarship. He is the literary executor for Margaret Archer, and he works to promote engagement with her morphogenetic approach, particularly amongst early career researchers.
